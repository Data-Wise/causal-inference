🧭 Introduction: The Problem of Confounding

Welcome to Week 3 of STAT 579. This week, we will tackle the central challenge of causal inference in observational studies: confounding. The goal is to move from simply observing an association to confidently making a causal claim. We will learn a rigorous, graphical method—the backdoor criterion—to identify the variables we need to adjust for to achieve this goal.

Our journey this week connects the potential outcomes framework we learned in Week 2 with the causal graphical models (DAGs) that we will use throughout the semester. By the end of this module, you will be able to define confounding, use DAGs to find an appropriate adjustment set, and implement this logic in R code.

🧠 Conceptual Foundations: Confounding and Exchangeability

The Definition of a Confounder

A confounder is a variable that is a common cause of both the treatment and the outcome. This creates a non-causal association between the treatment and outcome that can be mistaken for a true causal effect.

🧠 Learning Tip: Two Frameworks, One Problem

The concept of confounding can be understood through two lenses:

  1. Graphical Framework (DAGs): Confounding is visually represented by a backdoor path. A backdoor path is a sequence of arrows that connects the treatment (\(A\)) to the outcome (\(Y\)) and begins with an arrow pointing into the treatment node. For example, in the DAG \(A \leftarrow C \rightarrow Y\), the path \(A \leftarrow C \rightarrow Y\) is a backdoor path. The variable \(C\) is a confounder.

What is Being “Exchanged”?

In causal inference, when we say two groups are exchangeable, we mean that they are comparable in every way relevant to the outcome. Specifically, if we were to hypothetically exchange the treatment assignment between the two groups, the distribution of their potential outcomes would remain the same.

The key misunderstanding to avoid is thinking we are exchanging the individuals themselves, or the treatments. Instead, we are making an assumption about the comparability of the groups at baseline.

The concept of exchangeability applies to the potential outcomes, not the observed outcomes or the treatments themselves.

Exchangeability and Potential Outcomes

Let’s break this down using our notation from Week 2:

  • \(Y^1\): The potential outcome if an individual receives treatment (\(A=1\)).
  • \(Y^0\): The potential outcome if an individual receives no treatment (\(A=0\)).

When we say the treatment group (\(A=1\)) and the control group (\(A=0\)) are unconditionally exchangeable, we are making the following two assumptions:

  1. The control group is a valid proxy for what would have happened to the treated group if they had not been treated. This means the average potential outcome of the treated group under no treatment, \(\E{Y^0 \mid A=1}\), is equal to the average potential outcome of the control group under no treatment, \(\E{Y^0 \mid A=0}\). The groups are identical in their potential outcomes under the control condition.

  2. The treated group is a valid proxy for what would have happened to the control group if they had been treated. This means the average potential outcome of the treated group under treatment, \(\E{Y^1 \mid A=1}\), is equal to the average potential outcome of the control group under treatment, \(\E{Y^1 \mid A=0}\). The groups are identical in their potential outcomes under the treatment condition.

The full assumption of unconditional exchangeability is captured by the single formula: \[ Y^a \indep A \quad \text{for } a=0,1 \]

In plain English: “The potential outcome for a given treatment level ‘a’ is independent of whether a unit actually received that treatment.” This is the foundation of a randomized controlled trial (RCT), where the random assignment ensures these two distributions are, on average, identical.

Conditional Exchangeability

In an observational study, we know this unconditional assumption is usually false. However, we can still achieve conditional exchangeability if we can identify and adjust for all confounders (\(\vect{C}\)). This means the two groups are comparable within levels of the confounders.

The formula we use is: \[ Y^a \indep A \mid \vect{C} \]

In plain English: “The potential outcome for a given treatment level ‘a’ is independent of whether a unit actually received that treatment, once we have accounted for the variables in \(\vect{C}\).” This allows us to make causal claims from observational data, as long as this assumption holds.

The Goal: Achieving Exchangeability

To estimate a causal effect, we need the treatment and control groups to be exchangeable.

  • Unconditional Exchangeability: This is the ideal state, often achieved in a randomized controlled trial (RCT). The randomization ensures that treatment assignment (\(A\)) is independent of the potential outcomes (\(Y^a\)) for all units. That is, \(A \indep Y^a\). This means the treated and untreated groups are, on average, identical on all factors (measured and unmeasured) that influence the outcome.
  • Conditional Exchangeability: In observational studies, we can’t randomize. Instead, we can assume that we have measured all the confounders. If we adjust for this set of measured confounders, say \(\vect{C}\), then within each stratum of \(\vect{C}\), the treatment assignment is “as good as random.” This assumption is formally stated as:

\[ Y^a \indep A \mid \vect{C} \]

This is the central assumption that allows us to move from an observed association to a causal conclusion. The key challenge, then, is to identify the correct set of variables \(\vect{C}\) to adjust for.

Unconditional Exchangeability in an RCT

Let’s illustrate the concept of unconditional exchangeability with a simple numerical example. Imagine we have a population of 200 patients and a new drug we believe will cure a disease. For each patient, there are two potential outcomes: the outcome if they take the drug (\(Y^1\)) and the outcome if they don’t (\(Y^0\)). A value of 1 means they are cured, and 0 means they are not.

In a perfect world, we would observe both potential outcomes for every person, as shown in the table below. The true causal effect for each person is the difference \(Y^1 - Y^0\). The Average Treatment Effect (ATE) is the average of these differences across the population.

Patient Potential Outcome (\(Y^1\)) Potential Outcome (\(Y^0\)) Individual Causal Effect
1 1 0 +1
2 1 1 0
… … … …
200 … … …
Average 0.6 0.4 +0.2

The true ATE in this population is 0.2 (20% of patients are cured by the drug). But, in reality, we can only observe one of these potential outcomes for each person.

Now, let’s simulate an RCT where we randomly assign 100 patients to the treatment group (\(A=1\)) and 100 to the control group (\(A=0\)). Because of random assignment, the two groups are, on average, identical. This is the essence of unconditional exchangeability.

Group N Average Potential Outcome (\(Y^1\)) Average Potential Outcome (\(Y^0\)) Observed Outcome (Observed \(Y\))
Treated (\(A=1\)) 100 \(\E{Y^1 \mid A=1}\) \(\E{Y^0 \mid A=1}\) \(\E{Y^1 \mid A=1}\)
Control (\(A=0\)) 100 \(\E{Y^1 \mid A=0}\) \(\E{Y^0 \mid A=0}\) \(\E{Y^0 \mid A=0}\)
Population 200 \(\E{Y^1}\) \(\E{Y^0}\)

Because the two groups are exchangeable, their potential outcome distributions are the same. This means:

  • \[\E{Y^1 \mid A=1} = \E{Y^1 \mid A=0} = \E{Y^1} = 0.6\]
  • \[\E{Y^0 \mid A=1} = \E{Y^0 \mid A=0} = \E{Y^0} = 0.4\]

The magic of randomization is that it allows us to use the observed outcomes to estimate the potential outcomes of the other group.

  • The observed average outcome in the treated group, \(\E{Y \mid A=1}\), gives us an unbiased estimate of the average potential outcome for the entire population under treatment, \(\E{Y^1}\). \[ \E{Y \mid A=1} = \E{Y^1 \mid A=1} \approx \E{Y^1} = 0.6 \]

  • The observed average outcome in the control group, \(\E{Y \mid A=0}\), gives us an unbiased estimate of the average potential outcome for the entire population under no treatment, \(\E{Y^0}\). \[ \E{Y \mid A=0} = \E{Y^0 \mid A=0} \approx \E{Y^0} = 0.4 \]

The observed difference in our data is \(\E{Y \mid A=1} - \E{Y \mid A=0} = 0.6 - 0.4 = 0.2\). This observed difference is an unbiased estimate of the true ATE, because of the exchangeability created by randomization.

🧠 The Backdoor Criterion: Finding the Right Adjustment Set

The backdoor criterion is a formal rule, derived from the theory of DAGs, that tells us precisely which set of variables to adjust for to achieve conditional exchangeability.

The Criterion

A set of variables \(\vect{C}\) satisfies the backdoor criterion relative to an exposure \(A\) and an outcome \(Y\) if:

  1. It blocks every backdoor path from \(A\) to \(Y\).
  2. It does not contain any variable that is a descendant of \(A\) (this prevents us from conditioning on a mediator, which we will discuss later).

A backdoor path from \(A\) to \(Y\) is any path that connects \(A\) to \(Y\) with an arrow pointing into \(A\).

How to Block a Path

Blocking a path is a key concept. A path is blocked if it contains:

  • A collider (O) that we have not conditioned on.
  • A non-collider (C) that we have conditioned on.

A collider (O) is a variable on a path where two arrows point into it (e.g., \(X \to O \leftarrow Y\)).

Therefore, to block a backdoor path, we must adjust for a non-collider (C) on that path.

Comparison to the Potential Outcomes Framework

The backdoor criterion provides the bridge between our two frameworks. The assumption of conditional exchangeability (\(Y^a \indep A \mid \vect{C}\)) is the potential outcomes condition for identifiability. The backdoor criterion is the graphical rule for finding a set of variables \(\vect{C}\) that, if correctly measured and controlled for, makes the conditional exchangeability assumption plausible.

🧠 “Bad Controls”: Variables to Avoid

Not all variables should be adjusted for. In fact, adjusting for the wrong variable can create bias where there was none before. These are often called “bad controls.”

Common Pitfall: Adjusting for Colliders

Consider the DAG \(A \to Y \leftarrow O \to C\). In this case, \(O\) is a collider on the path between \(A\) and \(C\). There is no backdoor path from \(A\) to \(Y\). However, if we condition on \(O\) (e.g., by including it in a regression model), we “open” the path through the collider, creating a spurious association between \(A\) and \(Y\).

For example, imagine \(A\) is talent and \(Y\) is physical attractiveness, which are independent. We study their effect on fame (\(O\)). If we only look at people who are famous (conditioning on \(O\)), we will find that less attractive people must have more talent to be famous, and vice versa. This creates a spurious, non-causal association between talent and attractiveness.

  • Mediators: A mediator (\(M\)) lies on the causal pathway from \(A\) to \(Y\) (i.e., \(A \to M \to Y\)). If we are interested in the total effect of \(A\) on \(Y\), we should not adjust for the mediator, as this would block a part of the causal effect we are trying to measure. However, if we want to estimate the direct effect of \(A\) on \(Y\) (holding \(M\) constant), we would need to adjust for it, which is a different estimand entirely.
  • Confounders: Adjusting for confounders is necessary.
  • Colliders: Adjusting for colliders is bad.
  • Mediators: Adjusting for mediators is bad if you want the total effect.

✅ Detailed Numerical and R Example

Let’s use the example from the lecture notes and walk through it step-by-step, including the corresponding R code.

The Scenario

We are studying the effect of a new blood pressure medication (\(A\)) on heart disease mortality (\(Y\)). We believe that age (\(C\)) is a confounder, as older patients are more likely to be prescribed the medication and are also more likely to die from heart disease.

The DAG is: \(A \leftarrow C \rightarrow Y\) and \(A \rightarrow Y\).

  • Backdoor Path: \(A \leftarrow C \rightarrow Y\).
  • Adjustment Set: To block this path, we must adjust for \(C\), as it is the only non-collider on the path.
  • Minimal Adjustment Set: The minimal sufficient adjustment set is \(\vect{C}\). The set contains all variables that are not colliders (or its descendants) nor mediators (or its descendants) on any backdoor paths from \(A\) to \(Y\).

Step-by-Step Calculation

We have the following data from 200 patients:

Group Treatment (\(A\)) Mortality (\(Y\)) Total
Young (\(C=0\)) \(A=1\) 10 40
\(A=0\) 5 20
Old (\(C=1\)) \(A=1\) 40 60
\(A=0\) 20 80

  1. Crude Association: The unadjusted association between treatment and mortality.

    • Total treated (\(A=1\)): \(40+60 = 100\). Deaths: \(10+40=50\). Crude mortality: \(50/100 = 50%\).
    • Total untreated (\(A=0\)): \(20+80=100\). Deaths: \(5+20=25\). Crude mortality: \(25/100 = 25%\).
    • Crude risk difference: \(50% - 25% = 25%\). This suggests the drug increases mortality, but this is a biased estimate.

  2. Conditional Effects: We calculate the risk difference within each stratum of the confounder \(C\).

    • Young Group (\(C=0\)):
      • Risk with treatment: \(10/40 = 25\%\).
      • Risk without treatment: \(5/20 = 25\%\).
      • Conditional risk difference: \(25\% - 25\% = 0\%\).
    • Old Group (\(C=1\)):
      • Risk with treatment: \(40/60 = 66.7\%\).
      • Risk without treatment: \(20/80 = 25\%\).
      • Conditional risk difference: \(66.7\% - 25\% \approx 41.7\%\).

  3. Adjusted Estimate (Standardization): To get the Average Treatment Effect (ATE), we average the conditional effects across the marginal distribution of the confounder \(C\).

    • Marginal distribution of \(C\):
      • Total young: \(40+20=60\). Total old: \(60+80=140\).
      • \(\mathbb{P}(C=0) = 60/200 = 0.3\).
      • \(\mathbb{P}(C=1) = 140/200 = 0.7\).
    • The ATE is the weighted average of the stratum-specific effects:
      • \(\text{ATE} = \sum_z (\E{Y \mid A=1, C=z} - \E{Y \mid A=0, C=z}) \mathbb{P}(C=z)\)
      • \(\text{ATE} = (0\% \times 0.3) + (41.7\% \times 0.7) = 0 + 29.2\% = 29.2\%\).
    • The adjusted causal effect is a 29.2% increase in mortality, which is a significant difference from the crude estimate of 25%.

R Code Implementation

We can use glm to perform this adjustment. The key is to include the confounder C in the model.

# Load Necessary Libraries
# install.packages("dagitty")
# install.packages("dplyr")
library(dagitty)
library(dplyr)

# 1. Visualize the DAG and Identify the Adjustment Set
# Causal Structure: A <- C -> Y and A -> Y
dag <- dagitty("dag {
    A {exposure, pos=\"0,0\"};
    Y {outcome, pos=\"1,0\"};
    C {confounder, pos=\"0.5,1\"};
    C -> A;
    C -> Y;
    A -> Y;
}")
plot(dag)

# The Backdoor Path is A <- C -> Y.
# We Need to Block This Path by Adjusting for C.
# The `adjustmentSets()` Function Will Confirm This.
adjustment_sets <- adjustmentSets(dag, exposure = "A", outcome = "Y", effect = "total")
print(adjustment_sets)
# Output Will Confirm that {C} is a Valid Adjustment Set.

# 2. Simulate Data Based on This Causal Structure
set.seed(579) # For reproducibility
n <- 200
C <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.7) # Confounder (0=young, 1=old)
A <- rbinom(n, 1, prob = case_when(C == 0 ~ 0.4, C == 1 ~ 0.6)) # Treatment depends on confounder
Y <- rbinom(n, 1, prob = case_when(A == 0 & C == 0 ~ 0.25, A == 1 & C == 0 ~ 0.25,
                                   A == 0 & C == 1 ~ 0.25, A == 1 & C == 1 ~ 0.667)) # Outcome depends on A and C
data <- data.frame(A, Y, C)

# 3. Perform the Crude (unadjusted) Analysis Using a Logistic Regression
# This Model Ignores the Confounder C.
crude_model <- glm(Y ~ A, data = data, family = binomial(link = "logit"))
summary(crude_model)
cat("Crude odds ratio for A:", exp(coef(crude_model){"A"}), "\n")
# The Coefficient for A Will Be Biased, Reflecting the Confounded Association.

# 4. Perform the Adjusted Analysis by including the Confounder C
# This Model Blocks the Backdoor Path.
adjusted_model <- glm(Y ~ A + C, data = data, family = binomial(link = "logit"))
summary(adjusted_model)
cat("Adjusted odds ratio for A (within strata of C):", exp(coef(adjusted_model){"A"}), "\n")
# The Coefficient for A in This Model is an Estimate of the Conditional Causal Effect.
# Note: For Logistic Regression, This is the Conditional Odds Ratio. To Get the
# ATE (risk difference), We Need to Use More Advanced Methods like G-computation,
# Which We Will Cover in a Later Lecture. For Now, the Key is that This Model
# Successfully Blocks the Backdoor Path and Provides an Unbiased Estimate
# Of the *conditional* Causal Effect, Which is the Log-odds Ratio Here.

❓ Check Your Understanding

  1. Question: In an observational study, if we are certain that all confounders are measured, what core assumption of the potential outcomes framework are we trying to satisfy?
    • Answer: You are trying to satisfy the conditional exchangeability assumption, \(Y^a \indep A \mid X\).
  2. Question: Draw a DAG with nodes \(A\), \(Y\), \(M\), and \(C\), where \(C\) is a confounder and \(M\) is a mediator. Write down the paths from \(A\) to \(Y\). What is the minimal sufficient adjustment set for the total effect of \(A\) on \(Y\)?
    • Answer: The DAG is \(A \leftarrow C \rightarrow Y\) and \(A \rightarrow M \rightarrow Y\). The paths from \(A\) to \(Y\) are:
      1. \(A \to Y\) (the direct causal effect)
      2. \(A \to M \to Y\) (the indirect causal effect)
      3. \(A \leftarrow C \rightarrow Y\) (the backdoor confounding path)
    • The minimal sufficient adjustment set is \({C}\) because it is the only variable on the backdoor path that is not a descendant of \(A\). We should not adjust for \(M\) because we are interested in the total effect.
  3. Question: A study on the effect of exercise (\(A\)) on blood pressure (\(Y\)) adjusts for both diet (\(C\)) and a genetic marker (\(O\)). The DAG shows \(A \leftarrow C \rightarrow Y\), \(A \to O \leftarrow Y\). Based on this DAG, is adjusting for \(O\) a good idea? Why or why not?
    • Answer: No, adjusting for \(O\) is a bad idea. In this DAG, \(O\) is a collider on the path between \(A\) and \(Y\). Conditioning on a collider opens a non-causal path, which can induce a spurious association and bias your estimate. You should only adjust for the confounder \(C\).
