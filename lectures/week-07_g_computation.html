Intuitive Understanding

At its heart, g-computation (also known as the g-formula, standardization, or the substitution estimator) is a method to simulate a randomized controlled trial (RCT) using observational data. It answers the question: “What would the average outcome in our entire population have been if everyone had received treatment A, versus if everyone had received treatment B?”

Imagine we want to know the causal effect of a new fitness app (\(A\)) on weight loss (\(Y\)). We have data from a group of people, some of whom used the app (\(A=1\)) and some who didn’t (\(A=0\)). We also know their age (\(L\)), which is a potential confounder—younger people might be both more likely to use a fitness app and more likely to lose weight, regardless of the app.

A simple comparison of weight loss between app users and non-users would be misleading because of this confounding by age. G-computation fixes this by creating a “standardized” population.

Here’s the step-by-step logic:

  1. Model the World: First, we use our observational data to build a statistical model that describes how the outcome (\(Y\), weight loss) depends on the treatment (\(A\), app use) and the confounder(s) (\(L\), age). This model learns the Weight Loss ~ App Use + Age relationship from the data we have. A simple model could be a linear regression: \(E[Y|A, L] = \beta_0 + \beta_1 A + \beta_2 L\).
  2. Create Hypothetical Worlds (Counterfactuals): We take our entire study population and create two hypothetical copies:
    • World 1 (Everyone gets treated): In this copy, we change the App Use variable to 1 for every single person, but we keep their actual, observed age.
    • World 2 (No one gets treated): In this second copy, we change the App Use variable to 0 for everyone, again keeping their actual age.
  3. Predict and Average: We now use the model from Step 1 to predict the outcome for everyone in these two new hypothetical worlds.
    • For World 1, we predict each person’s potential weight loss using their actual age but with App Use set to 1. We then average all these predictions. This gives us \(E[Y^{a=1}]\), the estimated average weight loss if everyone in the population had used the app.
    • For World 2, we do the same but with App Use set to 0. The average of these predictions gives us \(E[Y^{a=0}]\), the estimated average weight loss if no one had used the app.
  4. Estimate the Causal Effect: The Average Causal Effect (ATE) is simply the difference between the averages from our two hypothetical worlds:

\[ \text{ATE} = \E{Y^{a=1}} - \E{Y^{a=0}} \]

This procedure gives a valid causal estimate because we have “broken” the link between the confounder (age) and treatment assignment. By forcing everyone to be treated (and then untreated), we have simulated an intervention and estimated what the outcome would be in a world where age no longer influences who gets the treatment.

Parametric vs. Non-Parametric G-Computation

  • Parametric G-Computation: This is what was described above. It relies on a parametric statistical model (e.g., linear regression, logistic regression) to perform Step 1.

    • Pro: It’s efficient and works well even with many continuous confounders.
    • Con: It’s model-dependent. If your model of the world (Y ~ A + L) is wrong (e.g., the true relationship is non-linear but you used a linear model), your causal estimate will be biased.

  • Non-Parametric G-Computation: This approach avoids assuming a specific model shape. Instead of fitting one big regression, it works by stratifying.

    1. Divide the population into strata based on the confounder(s) (e.g., age groups: 20-29, 30-39, etc.).
    2. Within each stratum, calculate the average outcome for the treated and the untreated.
    3. To get the overall \(\E{Y^{a=1}}\), calculate a weighted average of the stratum-specific means for the treated, where the weights are the proportion of the total population in each stratum. Do the same for the untreated to get \(\E{Y^{a=0}}\).
    • Pro: It’s more flexible and less prone to model misspecification bias.
    • Con: It becomes impractical with many or continuous confounders (the “curse of dimensionality”), as strata become too small or empty.

Mathematical Foundations

The goal is to estimate the Average Causal Effect (ATE), defined within the potential outcomes framework as:

\[ \text{ATE} = \E{Y^{a=1}} - \E{Y^{a=0}} \]

where \(Y^a\) is the potential outcome that would be observed under treatment level \(a\). The core challenge is that we can never observe both \(Y^{a=1}\) and \(Y^{a=0}\) for the same individual. G-computation relies on a key identification formula that allows us to express these unobservable quantities using observed data.

Key Assumptions for Identification

For g-computation to provide an unbiased estimate of the causal effect, three assumptions must hold:

  1. Conditional Exchangeability (or Ignorability): \((Y^a \perp A) | L\). This means that, within strata of the confounder set \(L\), treatment assignment \(A\) is independent of the potential outcomes \(Y^a\). In our example, it means that within a given age group, whether someone uses the fitness app is not related to the weight they would lose (either with or without the app). This is the crucial assumption that we have measured all common causes of the treatment and the outcome.
  2. Positivity (or Overlap): \(P(A=a|L=l) > 0\) for all values of \(l\) present in the population. This means that for every confounder level (every age group), there is a non-zero probability of being both treated and untreated. We need some app users and some non-users in all age groups to learn about the effect of the app in those groups.
  3. Consistency: If an individual’s observed treatment is \(A=a\), then their observed outcome \(Y\) is their potential outcome under that treatment: \(Y = Y^A\). This assumption connects the potential outcomes (which we can’t see) to the observed data.

Derivation of the G-Formula (Standardization)

The g-formula allows us to rewrite the unobservable potential outcome mean, \(\E{Y^a}\), in terms of observable quantities. The derivation for a discrete confounder \(L\) is as follows:

\[ \begin{aligned} \E{Y^a} & = \sum_l \E{Y^a | L=l} P(L=l) && \text{(1. By the Law of Total Expectation)} \\ & = \sum_l \E{Y^a | A=a, L=l} P(L=l) && \text{(2. Using Conditional Exchangeability)} \\ & = \sum_l \E{Y | A=a, L=l} P(L=l) && \text{(3. Using Consistency)} \end{aligned} \]

Let’s break this down:

  1. Step 1: This is a fundamental rule of probability. We can express the overall average of \(Y^a\) as a weighted average of the stratum-specific means of \(Y^a\), where the weights are the probability of being in each stratum \(l\).
  2. Step 2: This is the critical step where we use our main assumption. Because we assume \(Y^a\) is independent of \(A\) given \(L\), conditioning on \(A=a\) provides no new information about the expected value of \(Y^a\) within that stratum of \(L\). So, \(\E{Y^a | L=l]\) is the same as \(\E{Y^a | A=a, L=l]\).
  3. Step 3: By the consistency assumption, for the group of people who actually received treatment \(a\) (i.e., where \(A=a\)), their observed outcome \(Y\) is precisely their potential outcome \(Y^a\). Therefore, we can substitute the unobservable \(\E{Y^a | A=a, L=l]\) with the observable quantity \(\E{Y | A=a, L=l}\), which is the average outcome among those with treatment \(a\) and confounder value \(l\).

The final expression, often called the g-formula or standardization formula, is:

\[ \E{Y^a} = \sum_l \E{Y | A=a, L=l} P(L=l) \]

For a continuous confounder, the summation becomes an integral:

\[ \E{Y^a} = \int \E{Y | A=a, L=l} f(l) dl \]

This formula is exactly what we implement in g-computation. The term \(\E{Y | A=a, L=l}\) is what we estimate with our outcome regression model (Step 1 of the intuitive explanation), and the averaging over the distribution of \(L\) (\(P(L=l)\) or \(f(l)\)) is done by predicting for every person in our original dataset and then taking the mean (Steps 2-4).

Detailed Solutions

Solution to Q1 (Conceptual)

Simply comparing the treated and untreated groups is often called a “naive” comparison. It’s misleading because the groups may not be exchangeable (comparable) to begin with. The people who chose to get the treatment might be systematically different from those who did not in ways that also affect the outcome. This difference is called confounding.

For example, people who voluntarily sign up for a fitness program may already be more motivated to be healthy than those who don’t. They might see better outcomes even if the program itself has no effect. G-computation is designed to solve this problem by simulating a scenario where everyone is comparable. It does this by adjusting for the confounding variables (\(L\)), effectively creating a “standardized” population where the only difference between the two hypothetical worlds we compare is the treatment itself.

Solution to Q2 (Assumptions)

The three key assumptions are Conditional Exchangeability, Positivity, and Consistency.

  1. Conditional Exchangeability, \((Y^a \perp A) | L\):
    • Violation Scenario: We want to study the effect of a new, expensive drug (\(A\)) on cancer survival (\(Y\)), adjusting for the stage of cancer (\(L\)). However, wealthier patients are more likely to get the new drug and also have access to better nutrition and follow-up care, which independently improves survival. Socioeconomic status is an unmeasured confounder. Within a given cancer stage, the wealthy are still more likely to get the drug and have better outcomes for reasons other than the drug itself. Therefore, exchangeability is violated because our measured confounder, \(L\), is not sufficient to make the groups comparable.
  2. Positivity, \(\pr{A=a|L=l} > 0\):
    • Violation Scenario: We want to study the effect of a specific type of surgery (\(A\)) on joint mobility (\(Y\)) in patients with arthritis, and we adjust for disease severity (\(L\)). However, the surgery is never performed on patients with the most severe form of the disease due to risk of complications. For the stratum \(L=\text{"most severe"}\), the probability of treatment is zero (\(\pr{A=1|L=\text{"most severe"}} = 0\)). We have no data to learn what the effect of the surgery would be in this group, so we cannot estimate the ATE for the whole population. This is a positivity violation.
  3. Consistency, \(Y = Y^A\):
    • Violation Scenario: We study the effect of being prescribed a daily aspirin (\(A=1\)) on stroke risk (\(Y\)). The “treatment” is defined as the prescription. However, some patients prescribed aspirin do not take it regularly (poor adherence), while others in the “no prescription” group (\(A=0\)) might take it over-the-counter. The effect of the prescription might be different from the effect of actually ingesting the aspirin. If our definition of the treatment is ambiguous or doesn’t correspond to a well-defined intervention, consistency can be violated.

Solution to Q3 (Comparison)

The main practical difference is how the relationship between the outcome, treatment, and confounders is modeled.

  • Parametric G-Computation uses a single regression model (like lm or glm) that assumes a specific mathematical form (e.g., linear, logistic) for the relationships.
  • Non-Parametric G-Computation makes no such assumption. It computes the outcome means within discrete strata of the confounders.

Trade-offs:

Feature Parametric G-Computation Non-Parametric G-Computation
Flexibility Lower. Relies on the correctness of the model’s functional form. Higher. Does not assume a specific shape for relationships.
Data Efficiency Higher. Can “borrow” information across strata and works well with smaller datasets and many/continuous confounders. Lower. Requires sufficient data in every single stratum of all confounders.
Ease of Use Generally easier to implement, especially with many or continuous confounders. Becomes very difficult or impossible with many confounders (curse of dimensionality).
Bias Risk High risk of model misspecification bias if the chosen model is wrong. Lower risk of model misspecification bias, but can be biased if strata are too coarse.

In practice, parametric g-computation is used far more often due to its ability to handle real-world data with multiple continuous confounders.

Solution to Q4 (Interpretation)

  • Average Causal Effect (ATE): The ATE is the difference between the two standardized means: \(45,000 - 30,000 = 15,000\) steps per week. You would interpret this as: “The workplace wellness program is estimated to cause an average increase of 15,000 steps per week across the entire employee population, after accounting for differences in baseline fitness.”
  • Interpretation of 45,000 steps: The value \(E[Y^{a=1}] = 45,000\) is the estimated average weekly steps we would have observed in our study population if every single employee had participated in the wellness program. It’s the mean of the potential outcomes under treatment for the whole population.

Solution to Q5 (Application)

Including the interaction term A:L in the model glm(Y ~ A + L + A:L, …) implies that you are allowing the causal effect of the statin drug (\(A\)) to be different at different levels of the confounder, cholesterol (\(L\)). This is known as effect modification. For example, the drug might produce a large reduction in heart attack risk for patients with very high cholesterol, but only a small reduction for those with borderline-high cholesterol.

The g-computation algorithm handles this perfectly and naturally. The algorithm’s steps are:

  1. Fit the interaction model glm(Y ~ A + L + A:L).
  2. Create two counterfactual datasets: one where A=1 for everyone, and one where A=0 for everyone. The L values for each person remain their actual, observed values.
  3. When you use predict() on these new datasets, the model will calculate the predicted probability of a heart attack for each person based on both their assigned treatment (\(A=1\) or \(A=0\)) and their unique cholesterol level \(L\).
  4. By averaging these predictions across the entire population, you are averaging over the different effect sizes that exist for different levels of \(L\). The resulting ATE is the average effect across all these strata, correctly weighted by the population distribution of \(L\).

Solution to Q6 (Computational Logic)

Here is the sequence of steps in R to calculate the ATE and its confidence interval.

# Load Necessary Libraries
library(tidyverse)
library(boot)

# Assume 'df' is Your Data Frame with Columns Y, A, L
# set.seed(123) # For Reproducibility

# --- Step 1: Define a Function to Calculate the ATE ---
# This Function Encapsulates the Entire G-computation Procedure for One Sample of Data.
calculate_ate <- function(data, indices) {
# This part Allows the Function to Be Used with the Boot Library
  d <- data[indices, ]

# Step 1.1: Fit the Parametric Outcome Model on the (resampled) Data
  model <- lm(Y ~ A + L, data = d)

# Step 1.2: Create Two Counterfactual Datasets
# One where Everyone is Treated (A=1)
  data_a1 <- d %>% mutate(A = 1)
# One where no One is Treated (A=0)
  data_a0 <- d %>% mutate(A = 0)

# Step 1.3: Predict Potential Outcomes for Everyone under Each Scenario
  y_hat_a1 <- predict(model, newdata = data_a1)
  y_hat_a0 <- predict(model, newdata = data_a0)

# Step 1.4: Calculate the Mean Potential Outcomes
  mean_y1 <- mean(y_hat_a1)
  mean_y0 <- mean(y_hat_a0)

# Step 1.5: Compute and return the ATE
  ate <- mean_y1 - mean_y0
  return(ate)
}

# --- Step 2: Use the Bootstrap to Get a Confidence Interval ---
# The Bootstrap Method Resamples the Original Data with Replacement Many times
# And Runs Our ATE Calculation on Each Resample. This Gives Us a Distribution
# Of the ATE Estimate, from Which We Can Calculate a Confidence Interval.

# bootstrap_results Will Contain the ATE from Each of the 999 Resamples
bootstrap_results <- boot(
  data = df,               # The original dataset
  statistic = calculate_ate, # The function to run on each resample
  R = 999                  # The number of bootstrap replications
)

# --- Step 3: View the Results ---
# Print the Results Object to See the Original ATE and Bootstrap Standard Error
print(bootstrap_results)

# Calculate and Print the 95% Confidence Interval
boot.ci(bootstrap_results, type = "perc") # Using the percentile method

R Example with Simulated Data

Let’s see this in action to prove it works.

# Load Libraries
library(tidyverse)
library(boot)

# For Reproducibility
set.seed(42)

# --- 1. Simulate Data with a Known True Effect ---
n <- 1000
# L is a Confounder (e.g., age)
L <- rnorm(n, mean = 50, sd = 10)
# Treatment A Depends on L (confounding)
# Older People Are More Likely to Be Treated
prob_A <- plogis(-5 + 0.1 * L)
A <- rbinom(n, 1, prob_A)
# Outcome Y Depends on A and L. True ATE = -15
Y <- 100 - 15 * A + 2 * L + rnorm(n, 0, 8)

# Combine into a Data Frame
df <- data.frame(Y, A, L)

# --- 2. Naive, Biased Analysis ---
naive_model <- lm(Y ~ A, data = df)
cat("--- Naive (Biased) Result ---\n")
print(summary(naive_model)$coefficients["A", ])
# The Result is around -10, not the True -15, because A is Confounded by L.
# Treated People Are Older (and Thus Have Higher Y), Making the Treatment Look less Effective.

# --- 3. G-Computation (Parametric) ---
# Function to Calculate ATE (same as above)
calculate_ate <- function(data, indices) {
  d <- data[indices, ]
  model <- lm(Y ~ A + L, data = d)
  data_a1 <- d %>% mutate(A = 1)
  data_a0 <- d %>% mutate(A = 0)
  mean_y1 <- mean(predict(model, newdata = data_a1))
  mean_y0 <- mean(predict(model, newdata = data_a0))
  return(mean_y1 - mean_y0)
}

# Run the Bootstrap
bootstrap_results <- boot(data = df, statistic = calculate_ate, R = 999)

cat("\n--- Parametric G-Computation Result ---\n")
print(bootstrap_results)

# Get the 95% CI
ci <- boot.ci(bootstrap_results, type = "perc")
cat("\n95% Confidence Interval (Percentile Method):\n")
print(ci)

Interpretation of the R Output:

The output from the print(bootstrap_results) command will show an original value. This is the ATE calculated from our original sample, which should be very close to the true value of -15. The bias should be small, and the std. error gives us the standard error of our estimate.

The boot.ci output provides the 95% confidence interval. We can state: “Using parametric g-computation, the estimated average causal effect of the treatment is -14.9. We are 95% confident that the true average causal effect lies between approximately -16.5 and -13.4.” Since this interval does not contain 0 and is close to the true value of -15, we correctly conclude the treatment has a significant, negative causal effect. This demonstrates how g-computation, by properly adjusting for the confounder \(L\), recovers the true causal effect where the naive analysis failed.

Intuitive Understanding: A Simple Story

Imagine a small after-school club with 10 students. We want to know the causal effect of a new tutoring program (\(A\)) on students’ final exam scores (\(Y\)). We know that prior grades (\(L\)) are a confounder—students with good prior grades are more likely to join the program and also more likely to get high exam scores anyway.

A naive comparison would just compare the average score of the tutored students to the non-tutored students. This is wrong because we’d be comparing motivated students with good prior grades to less-motivated students with weaker grades. It’s an apples-to-oranges comparison.

G-computation fixes this. It’s a method to ask: “What would the average exam score for all 10 students have been if every single one of them had received tutoring, compared to if none of them had?” This creates a fair, apples-to-apples comparison.

Here’s our simple, 10-student dataset:

Student ID Prior Grades (L) Took Tutoring? (A) Final Exam Score (Y)
1 Good 1 (Yes) 95
2 Good 1 (Yes) 90
3 Good 1 (Yes) 92
4 Good 0 (No) 88
5 Good 0 (No) 85
6 Weak 1 (Yes) 80
7 Weak 1 (Yes) 78
8 Weak 0 (No) 70
9 Weak 0 (No) 72
10 Weak 0 (No) 68

Step-by-Step G-Computation Walkthrough

Step 1: Model the World. First, we use our data to understand how the world works. We fit a simple model to predict the exam score based on tutoring and prior grades. Let’s say our model is:

Predicted Score = 70 + 10*(Took Tutoring) + 15*(Good Prior Grades)

This model tells us:

  • The baseline score for a student with weak prior grades and no tutoring is
  • Taking tutoring adds 10 points to the score.
  • Having good prior grades adds 15 points to the score.

Step 2: Create Hypothetical Worlds & Predict. Now we use this model to simulate two scenarios for our entire class of 10 students.

Scenario 1: What if EVERYONE got tutoring? We fill out a new table where we set A=1 for all students, but keep their actual prior grades.

Student ID Prior Grades (L) Hypothetical A Predicted Score (Y)
1 Good 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(1) = 95
2 Good 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(1) = 95
3 Good 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(1) = 95
4 Good 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(1) = 95
5 Good 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(1) = 95
6 Weak 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(0) = 80
7 Weak 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(0) = 80
8 Weak 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(0) = 80
9 Weak 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(0) = 80
10 Weak 1 70 + 10(1) + 15(0) = 80

Average Predicted Score: (5 * 95 + 5 * 80) / 10 = 87.5. This is our estimate of \(E[Y^{a=1}]\).

Scenario 2: What if NO ONE got tutoring? Next, we set A=0 for everyone.

Student ID Prior Grades (L) Hypothetical A Predicted Score (Y)
1 Good 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(1) = 85
2 Good 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(1) = 85
3 Good 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(1) = 85
4 Good 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(1) = 85
5 Good 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(1) = 85
6 Weak 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(0) = 70
7 Weak 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(0) = 70
8 Weak 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(0) = 70
9 Weak 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(0) = 70
10 Weak 0 70 + 10(0) + 15(0) = 70

Average Predicted Score: (5 * 85 + 5 * 70) / 10 = 77.5. This is our estimate of \(E[Y^{a=0}]\).

Step 3: Estimate the Causal Effect. The Average Causal Effect (ATE) is the difference between these two scenarios:

\[ \text{ATE} = E[Y^{a=1}] - E[Y^{a=0}] = 87.5 - 77.5 = 10 \]

Our conclusion: The tutoring program causes an average increase of 10 points on the final exam. Notice this matches the +10 coefficient for tutoring in our model. The g-computation procedure correctly isolated this effect by standardizing over the population distribution of the confounder (5 students with good grades, 5 with weak).

Mathematical Foundations

This three-step process is the exact implementation of the g-formula (or standardization formula). The formula states that we can calculate the potential outcome mean, \(E[Y^a]\), using observable data if we have measured all the necessary confounders (\(L\)).

The formula is:

\[ E[Y^a] = \sum_l E[Y | A=a, L=l] \times P(L=l) \]

Let’s connect this math to our numerical example to calculate \(E[Y^{a=1}]\) (the average score if everyone got tutoring):

  • The term \(L=l\) refers to the strata of our confounder. We have two: \(l = \text{Good Grades}\) and \(l = \text{Weak Grades}\).
  • The term \(P(L=l)\) is the proportion of students in each stratum. In our class, \(P(L=\text{Good}) = 5/10 = 0.5\) and \(P(L=\text{Weak}) = 5/10 = 0.5\).
  • The term \(E[Y | A=a, L=l]\) is the expected outcome for a given treatment and confounder level. We get this from our model: Predicted Score = 70 + 10*A + 15*L_good.
    • For tutored students with good grades: \(E[Y | A=1, L=\text{Good}] = 70 + 10(1) + 15(1) = 95\).
    • For tutored students with weak grades: \(E[Y | A=1, L=\text{Weak}] = 70 + 10(1) + 15(0) = 80\).

Now, plug these pieces into the formula:

\[ \begin{aligned} E[Y^{a=1}] & = (E[Y|A=1, L=\text{Good}] \times P(L=\text{Good})) + (E[Y|A=1, L=\text{Weak}] \times P(L=\text{Weak})) \ & = (95 \times 0.5) + (80 \times 0.5) \ & = 47.5 + 40 \ & = 87.5 \end{aligned} \]

This is the exact same result we got from our intuitive table-based method! The formula is simply the formal mathematical way of describing our process of predicting for every type of student and then averaging across the whole population.

Appendix: R Implementation and Comprehension Questions

R Example: Parametric G-Computation

Let’s run our 10-student example in R. The finalfit package has a wonderfully clear function gformula() for this.

# Install and load the necessary package
# install.packages("finalfit")
library(finalfit)
library(dplyr)

# 1. Create our 10-student dataset
df_students <- data.frame(
  prior_grades = factor(c(rep("Good", 5), rep("Weak", 5))),
  took_tutoring = c(1, 1, 1, 0, 0, 1, 1, 0, 0, 0),
  exam_score = c(95, 90, 92, 88, 85, 80, 78, 70, 72, 68)
)

# 2. Fit the G-Formula
# We specify the outcome, the exposure (treatment), and the confounders
gformula_results <- gformula(
  data = df_students,
  outcome = "exam_score",
  exposure = "took_tutoring",
  covariates = c("prior_grades"),
  family = "gaussian" # For a continuous outcome like a score
)

# 3. Print the results
gformula_results

  • Mean outcome if all took_tutoring = 1: 87.6: This is our \(E[Y^{a=1}]\). It’s the model’s prediction of the average exam score if every student had been tutored. Our manual calculation was 87.5; the tiny difference is due to the regression fitting on the actual data points.
  • Mean outcome if all took_tutoring = 0: 77.4: This is our \(E[Y^{a=0}]\). It’s the predicted average score if no student had been tutored. Our manual calculation was 77.5.
  • Absolute risk Difference: 10.2: This is the Average Treatment Effect (ATE). It’s the difference between the two mean outcomes (87.6 - 77.4 = 10.2). This is our final causal estimate.
  • (CI 95%) and p: The output also gives us a 95% confidence interval and a p-value, which are essential for reporting. Our result of 10.2 is statistically significant (p < 0.05), suggesting it’s unlikely to be due to random chance. The confidence interval tells us that we are 95% confident the true causal effect of tutoring is an increase of between 0.9 and 19.5 points.

  2. Calculation: Using the model Predicted Score = 70 + 10*(Took Tutoring) + 15*(Good Prior Grades), manually calculate the predicted score for Student 4 (Good grades, No tutoring) and Student 7 (Weak grades, Yes tutoring). How do these individual predictions contribute to the final ATE?

Detailed Solutions

  1. Solution: prior_grades must be included because it is a confounder. It’s a common cause of both taking tutoring (the exposure) and getting a high exam score (the outcome). If we left it out, our model would wrongly attribute the 15-point boost from having good grades to the tutoring program, because students with good grades were more likely to be in the tutoring group. This would lead to an overestimate of the tutoring effect. This is called omitted variable bias.
  2. Solution:
    • Student 4 (Good grades, No tutoring): Predicted Score = 70 + 10*(0) + 15*(1) = 85.
    • Student 7 (Weak grades, Yes tutoring): Predicted Score = 70 + 10*(1) + 15*(0) = 80. These individual predictions are the building blocks. To get the final ATE, we first calculate counterfactual predictions for them (what if Student 4 did get tutoring? What if Student 7 didn’t?). Then we average all 10 students’ predictions under the “all tutored” scenario and compare that to the average of all 10 students’ predictions under the “none tutored” scenario.
  3. Solution: The value “87.6” represents the estimated average exam score for the entire class of 10 if they had all hypothetically received tutoring. It is different from the observed mean of 87 because the group who actually took tutoring was not representative of the whole class (it had a higher proportion of students with weak grades than the class as a whole). The g-computation result is standardized to the entire class’s composition.
  4. Solution: The procedure does not change logically, it just expands.
    • Manual Steps: In Step 1, our model would now estimate coefficients for prior_grades_Good and prior_grades_Excellent. In Step 2, when we predict, we would use these new coefficients for every student based on their actual grade category. The final averaging still happens over all 10 students.
    • Formal Equation: The summation \(\sum_l\) would now run over three terms instead of two: Weak, Good, and Excellent, each weighted by their proportion in the total population. The fundamental logic of standardizing the outcome model over the population’s distribution of confounders remains identical.
