Week 10: Time-Varying Treatments & MSMs ⏳

Welcome, everyone. This week, we venture into one of the most complex and realistic scenarios in applied causal inference: analyzing longitudinal data where treatments, confounders, and outcomes are measured over time. Standard methods can fail spectacularly here. Our goal is to understand why they fail and to master a powerful tool for this challenge: the Marginal Structural Model (MSM).

1. Comprehensive Topic Explanation

In many longitudinal studies, a patient’s treatment in a given time interval may depend on their evolving health status. The challenge arises when that same health status is also affected by prior treatments. This creates a feedback loop that can severely bias our causal estimates if not handled correctly.

The Core Problem: Treatment-Confounder Feedback

Let’s consider a common clinical scenario. A patient with a chronic disease receives a treatment (\(A\)). Their response is monitored using a time-varying biomarker (\(L\)), such as viral load or blood pressure. The physician then uses the current value of \(L\) to decide whether to continue or modify treatment in the next period.

Here, the biomarker \(L\) is:

  1. A confounder for the next treatment’s effect, because it predicts both future treatment (\(A_{t+1}\)) and the final outcome (\(Y\)).
  2. A mediator of the previous treatment’s effect, as past treatment (\(A_t\)) affects the biomarker’s value (\(L_{t+1}\)), which in turn affects the outcome (\(Y\)).

This dual role is the essence of treatment-confounder feedback.

Let’s visualize this for two time points (\(t=0\) and \(t=1\)). Let \(L_0\) be a baseline confounder, \(A_0\) the initial treatment, \(L_1\) the time-varying confounder, \(A_1\) the second treatment, and \(Y\) the final outcome.

Look at the path \(A_0 \rightarrow L_1 \rightarrow A_1 \rightarrow Y\). \(L_1\) lies on this causal path. Now, consider the confounding of the \(A_1 \rightarrow Y\) relationship. There is a backdoor path \(A_1 \leftarrow L_1 \rightarrow Y\). To close this backdoor path and get an unbiased estimate of the effect of \(A_1\), conventional wisdom says we must “adjust for” or “condition on” \(L_1\).

However, conditioning on \(L_1\) is problematic. Because \(L_1\) is a descendant of \(A_0\) (it’s on the causal pathway from \(A_0\) to \(Y\)), conditioning on it blocks part of the total causal effect of the treatment sequence (\(A_0\), then \(A_1\)). As Hernán & Robins (2025, Part III) emphasize, standard adjustment methods like stratification or regression, which condition on \(L_1\), will produce biased estimates of the total effect of the treatment strategy.

The Solution: Marginal Structural Models (MSMs)

Instead of conditioning on the problematic variable \(L_1\), MSMs take a different approach. They use Inverse Probability of Treatment Weighting (IPW) to create a “pseudo-population” in which the arrows from the time-varying confounders (like \(L_1\)) into subsequent treatments (like \(A_1\)) are broken. In this pseudo-population, treatment assignment is no longer confounded by the prior covariate history, and we can directly estimate the causal effect using a simple weighted model.

The model is “marginal” because it models the marginal mean of the potential outcome, \(E[Y^{\bar{a}}]\), over the entire population, not conditional on the confounders. It is “structural” because it aims to estimate the causal effect of a specified treatment strategy.

A treatment strategy (or “regime”), denoted \(\bar{a} = (a_0, a_1, \dots, a_K)\), is a sequence of treatment decisions over the study period. The goal of an MSM is to compare the outcomes under different strategies, for example, the strategy “always treat” (\(\bar{a}=(1,1,\dots,1)\)) versus “never treat” (\(\bar{a}=(0,0,\dots,0)\)).

2. Intuitive Clarifications of Challenging Ideas

Analogy: Creating a Fair Race with Handicaps

Imagine a multi-stage race where, after each stage, healthier-looking runners are given a heavier backpack for the next stage. The organizers want to know the “causal effect” of always wearing a heavy backpack versus always wearing a light one. A naive comparison would be biased because the healthier runners (who are more likely to win anyway) are the ones assigned the heavy backpacks.

IPW is like re-weighting the runners’ results to simulate a fair race. A runner who was healthy but, against the odds, was given a light backpack gets a large weight. Their outcome now “counts for more” because they represent many other healthy runners who were forced to take heavy backpacks. Conversely, a less-healthy runner who (as expected) got a light backpack gets a smaller weight.

By creating these weights, we generate a pseudo-population of runners where the choice of backpack at each stage is independent of their health. In this new, weighted population, we can finally make a fair comparison. This is exactly what MSMs do, but with patient data instead of runners.

Sequential Exchangeability: The Key Assumption

For a single time-point study, we needed the assumption of exchangeability (\(Y^a \indep A \mid L\)) to control for confounding. For time-varying treatments, we need a stronger, sequential version of this assumption.

📌 Key Assumption: Sequential Exchangeability

Sequential exchangeability states that at every time point \(k\), the treatment \(A_k\) is independent of the potential outcomes \(Y^{\bar{a}}\), conditional on the observed past treatment history (\(\bar{A}_{k-1}\)) and past covariate history (\(\bar{L}_k\)). Formally:

\[ Y^{\bar{a}} \indep A_k \mid \bar{A}_{k-1} = \bar{a}_{k-1}, \bar{L}_k \]

for all strategies \(\bar{a}\) and time points \(k\).

This means that within any given history of past treatments and covariates, the specific treatment a person receives at time \(k\) is effectively random with respect to their ultimate potential outcome. It is the crucial assumption that allows us to use IPW to adjust for confounding over time.

3. Simple, Hand-Calculation Numerical Example(s)

Let’s solidify these ideas with a toy example. Consider a two-day study (\(k=0, 1\)) with 5 patients.

  • \(L_0=1\) for all (baseline, not a confounder here).
  • \(A_k\): Treatment at day \(k\) (1=Treated, 0=Untreated).
  • \(L_1\): Biomarker on day 1 (1=High, 0=Low). High is “bad”.
  • \(Y\): Outcome at day 2 (1=Recovered, 0=Not).

Our goal is to estimate the causal risk ratio of the strategy “always treat” (\(A_0=1, A_1=1\)) versus “never treat” (\(A_0=0, A_1=0\)).

Here is our observed data:

ID \(A_0\) \(L_1\) \(A_1\) \(Y\)
1 0 0 0 1
2 0 1 1 0
3 1 0 0 1
4 1 0 1 1
5 1 1 1 0

Step 1: Model Treatment Probabilities

We need to estimate \(\Prob{A_k=a_k \mid \bar{L}_k, \bar{A}_{k-1}}\).

  • For \(A_0\): It only depends on \(L_0\). Since \(L_0=1\) for everyone, \(\Prob{A_0=1} = 3/5 = 0.6\).
  • For \(A_1\): It depends on \(A_0\) and \(L_1\).
    • \(\Prob{A_1=1 \mid A_0=0, L_1=0} = 0/1 = 0\)
    • \(\Prob{A_1=1 \mid A_0=0, L_1=1} = 1/1 = 1\)
    • \(\Prob{A_1=1 \mid A_0=1, L_1=0} = 1/2 = 0.5\)
    • \(\Prob{A_1=1 \mid A_0=1, L_1=1} = 1/1 = 1\)

Step 2: Calculate Stabilized IP Weights (\(SW_i\))

The formula for the stabilized weight for an individual \(i\) is:

\[ SW_i = \prod_{k=0}^{K} \frac{\Prob{A_k=A_{i,k} \mid \bar{A}_{i, k-1}}}{\Prob{A_k=A_{i,k} \mid \bar{A}_{i, k-1}, \bar{L}_{i,k}}} \]

The numerator is the marginal probability of receiving the observed treatment at time \(k\), given past treatment history. The denominator is the probability conditional on past treatment and covariate history.

  • Numerator Models (marginal probability of treatment):
    • \(\Prob{A_0=1} = 3/5 = 0.6\). So \(\Prob{A_0=0}=0.4\).
    • \(\Prob{A_1=1 \mid A_0=0} = \frac{\text{ID=2}}{\text{ID=1,2}} = 1/2 = 0.5\).
    • \(\Prob{A_1=1 \mid A_0=1} = \frac{\text{ID=4,5}}{\text{ID=3,4,5}} = 2/3\).

Let’s calculate the weight for Patient 2: (\(A_0=0, A_1=1, L_1=1\))

  • Numerator (k=0): \(\Prob{A_0=0} = 0.4\)
  • Denominator (k=0): \(\Prob{A_0=0} = 0.4\) (no confounders at baseline)
  • Numerator (k=1): \(\Prob{A_1=1 \mid A_0=0} = 0.5\)
  • Denominator (k=1): \(\Prob{A_1=1 \mid A_0=0, L_1=1} = 1.0\)
  • \(SW_2 = \frac{0.4}{0.4} \times \frac{0.5}{1.0} = 0.5\)

Let’s calculate the weight for Patient 3: (\(A_0=1, A_1=0, L_1=0\))

  • Numerator (k=0): \(\Prob{A_0=1} = 0.6\)
  • Denominator (k=0): \(\Prob{A_0=1} = 0.6\)
  • Numerator (k=1): \(\Prob{A_1=0 \mid A_0=1} = 1/3\)
  • Denominator (k=1): \(\Prob{A_1=0 \mid A_0=1, L_1=0} = 1 - 0.5 = 0.5\)
  • \(SW_3 = \frac{0.6}{0.6} \times \frac{1/3}{0.5} \approx 0.67\)

You can calculate the weights for all patients this way:

5 1 1 1 0 \(P(A_0=1)P(A_1=1 | A_0=1) = 0.6 \times 2/3\) \(P(A_0=1)P(A_1=1 | A_0=1,L_1=1) = 0.6 \times 1.0\) 0.67

Step 3: Fit the Weighted Model

Now we fit a model for the outcome \(Y\) on treatments \(A_0\) and \(A_1\), weighted by \(SW_i\). The model is: \(\E{Y \mid A_0, A_1} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 A_0 + \beta_2 A_1\). We are interested in comparing the strategy \((A_0=1, A_1=1)\) vs \((A_0=0, A_1=0)\).

Risk under “Always Treat” (\(A_0=1, A_1=1\)): Only patients 4 and 5 followed this.

  • Weighted mean: \(\frac{\sum (Y_i \times SW_i)}{\sum SW_i} = \frac{(1 \times 1.33) + (0 \times 0.67)}{1.33 + 0.67} = \frac{1.33}{2.0} = 0.665\)

Risk under “Never Treat” (\(A_0=0, A_1=0\)): Only patient 1 followed this.

  • Weighted mean: \(\frac{\sum (Y_i \times SW_i)}{\sum SW_i} = \frac{1 \times 0.5}{0.5} = 1.0\)

Causal Risk Ratio (RR) = Risk(Always)/Risk(Never) = 0.665 / 1.0 = 0.665.

This hand-calculation shows how the weights create a pseudo-population where the effect of the treatment strategy can be estimated via a simple weighted average.

4. R Code Examples & Interpretation

Let’s work through a more realistic simulation. We’ll simulate data according to the DAG above, show that a naive model fails, and then correctly implement an MSM.

Simulation

Code 
set.seed(579)
n <- 1000 # number of patients
# Baseline confounder L0
L0 <- rnorm(n)
# Treatment at time 0 depends on L0
A0_prob <- plogis(-1 + L0)
A0 <- rbinom(n, 1, A0_prob)
# Time-varying confounder L1 depends on A0 and L0
L1 <- -0.5 * A0 + 0.8 * L0 + rnorm(n)
# Treatment at time 1 depends on L1, A0, and L0
A1_prob <- plogis(-1 + 0.6 * A0 + 1.2 * L1 + 0.5 * L0)
A1 <- rbinom(n, 1, A1_prob)
# Outcome Y depends on A0, A1, and L1
Y <- 1 + 2 * A0 + 2 * A1 - 0.9 * L1 + rnorm(n)

sim_data <- data.frame(id = 1:n, L0, A0, L1, A1, Y)
head(sim_data)

In this simulation, the true causal effect of \(A_0\) is +2 and \(A_1\) is +2. So, the average causal effect (ACE) of the strategy \((A_0=1, A_1=1)\) vs \((A_0=0, A_1=0)\) is 4.

The Naive (Biased) Model

A standard approach would be to regress \(Y\) on the treatments and the time-varying confounder.

Code 
naive_model <- lm(Y ~ A0 + A1 + L1, data = sim_data)
summary(naive_model)$coefficients

The estimate for A0 is 2.03 and for A1 is 1.98. Both are biased downwards. The A0 coefficient is particularly attenuated because we conditioned on a mediator (L1) of its effect.

MSM Step 1: Estimate Weights

We need to fit two logistic regression models for the treatment probabilities, one for \(A_0\) and one for \(A_1\).

Code 
# Denominator model for A0
den_mod_A0 <- glm(A0 ~ L0, data = sim_data, family = binomial)
# Denominator model for A1
den_mod_A1 <- glm(A1 ~ L1 + A0 + L0, data = sim_data, family = binomial)

# Numerator model for A0 (no past history)
num_mod_A0 <- glm(A0 ~ 1, data = sim_data, family = binomial)
# Numerator model for A1
num_mod_A1 <- glm(A1 ~ A0, data = sim_data, family = binomial)

# Get probabilities
sim_data$pd_A0 <- predict(den_mod_A0, type = "response")
sim_data$pd_A1 <- predict(den_mod_A1, type = "response")
sim_data$pn_A0 <- predict(num_mod_A0, type = "response")
sim_data$pn_A1 <- predict(num_mod_A1, type = "response")

Now, calculate the stabilized weight for each person at each time point.

Code 
# Weight for A0
sim_data$sw_A0 <- ifelse(
  sim_data$A0 == 1,
  sim_data$pn_A0 / sim_data$pd_A0,
  (1 - sim_data$pn_A0) / (1 - sim_data$pd_A0)
)
# Weight for A1
sim_data$sw_A1 <- ifelse(
  sim_data$A1 == 1,
  sim_data$pn_A1 / sim_data$pd_A1,
  (1 - sim_data$pn_A1) / (1 - sim_data$pd_A1)
)
# Final weight is the product
sim_data$sw <- sim_data$sw_A0 * sim_data$sw_A1

MSM Step 2: Fit the Weighted Model

We use geeglm from the geepack package. This is a generalization of glm that can account for repeated measures on the same subject (though in this specific simulation, we only have one outcome per subject, for more complex MSM setups it’s essential). We specify the weights using the weights argument.

Code 
# The model for the outcome Y does NOT include the confounder L1
# It is a marginal model
msm_fit <- geeglm(
  Y ~ A0 + A1,
  data = sim_data,
  id = id, # cluster by patient
  weights = sw,
  corstr = "independence"
)

summary(msm_fit)

Lab Task: HIV Treatment Strategy

We will use a simulated dataset (hiv_data.csv) that mimics a real-world cohort of HIV patients.

  • id: Patient identifier.
  • time: Visit number (0, 1, 2).
  • cd4: CD4 count at that visit (a time-varying confounder). A lower CD4 count is worse.
  • azt: Whether the patient started AZT therapy at that visit (1=Yes, 0=No). This is our time-varying treatment.
  • cd4_final: CD4 count at the end of the study (the outcome Y).

Goal: Estimate the causal effect of a strategy of “always starting AZT at the first opportunity” versus “never starting AZT”.

6. Common Pitfalls & Checks

Common Pitfall: Model Misspecification & Extreme Weights

The validity of your MSM depends entirely on the correct specification of the weight models. If your models for \(\Prob{A_k \mid \dots}\) are wrong, your weights will be wrong, and your final estimate will be biased.

A major warning sign is the presence of extreme weights. A very large weight for one individual means that their observed treatment history was extremely unlikely given their covariate history. This suggests a violation of the positivity assumption (i.e., that all patients have a non-zero probability of receiving any treatment history). These influential individuals can destabilize your results. Always check the distribution of your weights. If you see extreme weights, you may need to reconsider your model, or in some cases, truncate the weights.

Checking Balance

A key check is to see if your weighting scheme worked. In the pseudo-population (i.e., using the weights), the baseline covariates should not predict treatment assignment. We can check this using the cobalt package.

8. Appendix: Advanced Theory & Derivations

The Mathematics of Stabilized Weights

Our goal is to estimate the parameters of a marginal structural model, such as:

\[ \E{Y^{\bar{a}}} = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \sum_k a_k \]

where \(\bar{a} = (a_0, \dots, a_K)\) is a specific treatment strategy.

The problem is that in our observed data, the association between treatment \(\bar{A}\) and outcome \(Y\) is confounded. IPW creates a pseudo-population where this confounding is removed. The weight for an individual \(i\) is:

\[ W_i = \frac{1}{\prod_{k=0}^{K} \Prob{A_k=A_{i,k} \mid \bar{A}_{i,k-1}, \bar{L}_{i,k}}} \]

These are “unstabilized” weights. They have a mean of 1 but can have very high variance. High variance in weights leads to inefficient and unstable estimates.

Stabilized weights (\(SW_i\)) were developed to reduce this variance. The formula is:

\[ SW_i = \prod_{k=0}^{K} \frac{f(A_k \mid \bar{A}_{k-1})}{f(A_k \mid \bar{A}_{k-1}, \bar{L}_k)} \]

where \(f(\cdot)\) is the probability density or mass function. The denominator is the same as before: the probability of the observed treatment given past treatment and covariate history. The numerator, however, is new. It is the probability of the observed treatment given only past treatment history.

This numerator “stabilizes” the weights by pulling them back towards 1, reducing their variance. As shown by Hernán & Robins (2025, Chapter 21), fitting a weighted model with \(SW_i\) still yields a consistent estimator for the MSM parameters, provided the key assumptions (sequential exchangeability, positivity, consistency, no measurement error) hold and the models for the weights are correctly specified. The resulting estimator is more efficient than one using unstabilized weights.

Numeric Illustration

Consider Patient 3 from our hand-calculation example.

  • Observed history: \(A_0=1, A_1=0\).

  • Unstabilized Weight: \[ W_3 = \frac{1}{P(A_0=1) \times P(A_1=0 \mid A_0=1, L_1=0)} = \frac{1}{0.6 \times 0.5} = \frac{1}{0.3} \approx 3.33 \]

  • Stabilized Weight: \[ SW_3 = \frac{P(A_0=1) \times P(A_1=0 \mid A_0=1)}{P(A_0=1) \times P(A_1=0 \mid A_0=1, L_1=0)} = \frac{0.6 \times (1/3)}{0.6 \times 0.5} = \frac{0.2}{0.3} \approx 0.67 \]

Note how the stabilized weight is much smaller and less extreme than the unstabilized version. This leads to more stable and precise final estimates.
