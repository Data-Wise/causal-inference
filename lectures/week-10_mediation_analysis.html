    Causal Mediation Analysis: Natural Direct and Indirect Effects

    Overview

    Causal mediation analysis (CMA) addresses a fundamental scientific question: how does a treatment or exposure produce its effect on an outcome? Rather than merely establishing that \(A\) causes \(Y\), mediation analysis decomposes the total effect into components that flow through an intermediate variable (the mediator \(M\)) versus those that bypass it. This module introduces the potential outcomes framework for mediation, defines natural direct effects (NDE) and natural indirect effects (NIE), and explores the critical sequential ignorability assumption required for identification.

    We begin by critiquing traditional approaches (e.g., Baron & Kenny’s product-of-coefficients method) and demonstrate how the counterfactual framework offers a model-free definition of mediation effects. Students will learn to work with nested counterfactuals (\(Y^{a, M^a}\)), understand the composition assumption, and apply sequential ignorability to derive identifiable estimands. The module integrates directed acyclic graphs (DAGs), hand calculations, and R implementations using dagitty, ggdag, and the mediation package.

    By the end of this module, you will be equipped to critically evaluate mediation claims, recognize confounding threats specific to mediation analysis, and conduct principled causal mediation studies in R.

    Learning Objectives

    By the end of this module, students will be able to:

    1. Articulate the limitations of traditional regression-based mediation approaches (Baron & Kenny) and explain why model-dependent definitions are problematic (30 minutes of study).
    2. Define natural direct effects (NDE), natural indirect effects (NIE), and controlled direct effects (CDE) using nested counterfactual notation and interpret each quantity in plain language (45 minutes).
    3. State the sequential ignorability assumption and identify which types of unmeasured confounding violate each component (20 minutes).
    4. Construct DAGs representing mediation scenarios with potential confounders and determine adjustment sets for identifying natural effects (40 minutes).
    5. Execute a complete causal mediation analysis in R, including data simulation, effect estimation, and sensitivity analysis (60 minutes of lab work).
    6. Distinguish between NDE, NIE, and CDE conceptually and numerically through hand calculations and software output (30 minutes).

    Comprehensive Topic Explanation

    The Quest for Mechanisms: Why Mediation Matters

    Scientific inquiry rarely stops at establishing that a treatment works. Researchers, policymakers, and clinicians want to understand why and how an intervention produces its effects. Does a literacy program improve children’s academic outcomes by increasing reading skills (\(M\)), by boosting self-confidence, or through some other pathway? Does a drug reduce mortality by lowering blood pressure, or through anti-inflammatory mechanisms? Mediation analysis provides a formal framework for answering these mechanistic questions (Hernán & Robins, 2020; Pearl, 2009).

    A mediator \(M\) is an intermediate variable that lies on the causal pathway from exposure \(A\) (or treatment \(T\)) to outcome \(Y\). The temporal ordering requirement is strict: \(A \to M \to Y\). Mediation analysis decomposes the total causal effect of \(A\) on \(Y\) into:

    • Indirect effect: The portion transmitted through \(M\).
    • Direct effect: The portion operating through all other pathways, excluding \(M\).

    Understanding mediation has practical implications. If the indirect effect dominates, interventions targeting the mediator may be effective. If the direct effect is large, alternative mechanisms deserve investigation.

    The Baron & Kenny Legacy: Regression-Based Mediation

    The traditional approach to mediation, popularized by Baron & Kenny (1986), involves fitting three regression models:

    1. \(Y \sim A + X\) (total effect, coefficient \(c\))
    2. \(M \sim A + X\) (exposure → mediator, coefficient \(a\))
    3. \(Y \sim A + M + X\) (outcome model, coefficients \(c'\) for \(A\) and \(b\) for \(M\))

    The indirect effect is estimated as \(\hat{a} \times \hat{b}\), and the direct effect as \(\hat{c}'\). Baron & Kenny proposed that “full mediation” occurs when \(c' = 0\) after controlling for \(M\).

    Limitations of this approach:

    • Model dependence: The definitions rely on specific functional forms (linear regression). With nonlinear models (logistic, Poisson), the product \(a \times b\) lacks clear causal interpretation (VanderWeele, 2015).
    • Unclear assumptions: What covariates \(X\) are needed? The approach doesn’t explicitly state conditions for causal interpretation.
    • Exposure-mediator interactions: When \(A \times M\) interactions exist, the decomposition becomes ambiguous.
    • Collider bias: Conditioning on \(M\) in model (3) can induce bias if \(M\) is affected by unmeasured factors that also influence \(Y\) (Pearl, 2014).

    These issues motivated the development of a counterfactual framework for mediation.

    Potential Outcomes: The Counterfactual Foundation

    The potential outcomes framework, also called the Rubin Causal Model or Neyman-Rubin framework, defines causation through hypothetical interventions (Holland, 1986; Rubin, 1974).

    Simple Potential Outcomes (No Mediator)

    For a binary exposure \(A \in \{0, 1\}\) and outcome \(Y\):

    • \(Y^{1}_i\): The outcome individual \(i\) would experience if \(A\) were set to 1 (treated).
    • \(Y^{0}_i\): The outcome individual \(i\) would experience if \(A\) were set to 0 (control).

    The individual causal effect is \(\tau_i = Y^{1}_i - Y^{0}_i\). We observe only \(Y_i = A_i Y^{1}_i + (1 - A_i)Y^{0}_i\); one potential outcome is always missing (the fundamental problem of causal inference).

    The average treatment effect (ATE) is:

    \[ \tau = \E{Y^{1} - Y^{0}} = \E{Y^{1}} - \E{Y^{0}} \]

    Under randomization of \(A\), \(\E{Y \mid A=1} = \E{Y^{1}}\) and \(\E{Y \mid A=0} = \E{Y^{0}}\), so the ATE is identified.

    1.3.2 Nested Counterfactuals for Mediation

    When a mediator \(M\) enters the picture, we need nested potential outcomes [Robins (2003); (vanderweele2009a?):

    • \(Y^{a, m}_i\): The outcome for individual \(i\) if \(A\) were set to \(a\) and \(M\) were set to \(m\).
    • \(M^{a}_i\): The mediator value for individual \(i\) if \(A\) were set to \(a\) (the potential mediator).

    The observed outcome is:

    \[ Y_i = Y^{A_i, M^{A_i}}_i \]

    This is the composition assumption: the outcome we observe is the potential outcome under the exposure value \(A_i\) and the mediator value that would naturally result from that exposure (Pearl, 2001).

    The total effect for individual \(i\) is:

    \[ \tau_i = Y^{1, M^{1}}_i - Y^{0, M^{0}}_i \]

    This compares the outcome when both \(A\) and \(M\) take their “treated” values versus when both take their “control” values.

    1.4 Natural Direct and Indirect Effects

    The key innovation of the counterfactual approach is defining mediation effects using cross-world counterfactuals—outcomes that combine exposure values from one “world” with mediator values from another (Pearl, 2001; Robins, 2003).

    1.4.1 Natural Indirect Effect (NIE)

    The natural indirect effect (NIE), also called the causal mediation effect, measures the impact of changing the mediator from its control value to its treated value, while holding the exposure fixed:

    \[ \delta^{a}_i = Y^{a, M^{1}}_i - Y^{a, M^{0}}_i \quad \text{for } a \in \{0, 1\} \]

    Interpretation: \(\delta^{a}_i\) is the change in \(Y\) induced by shifting \(M\) from the value it would naturally take under control (\(M^{0}_i\)) to the value it would take under treatment (\(M^{1}_i\)), while keeping \(A\) fixed at \(a\). If the exposure doesn’t affect the mediator (\(M^{1}_i = M^{0}_i\)), then \(\delta^{a}_i = 0\).

    The average NIE is:

    \[ \bar{\delta}(a) = \E{Y^{a, M^{1}} - Y^{a, M^{0}}} \]

    Typically, researchers report \(\bar{\delta}(0)\) (NIE when \(A=0\)) or \(\bar{\delta}(1)\) (NIE when \(A=1\)), or the average across both.

    1.4.2 Natural Direct Effect (NDE)

    The natural direct effect (NDE) captures the effect of changing the exposure while holding the mediator at the value it would naturally take under a reference exposure level:

    \[ \zeta^{a}_i = Y^{1, M^{a}}_i - Y^{0, M^{a}}_i \quad \text{for } a \in \{0, 1\} \]

    Interpretation: \(\zeta^{a}_i\) is the effect of \(A\) on \(Y\) through pathways other than \(M\), keeping \(M\) fixed at its potential value under exposure \(a\).

    The average NDE is:

    \[ \bar{\zeta}(a) = \E{Y^{1, M^{a}} - Y^{0, M^{a}}} \]

    Often, \(\bar{\zeta}(0)\) is called the “pure” natural direct effect (holding \(M\) at its control value).

    1.4.3 Decomposition of the Total Effect

    The total effect decomposes as:

    \[ \E{Y^{1, M^{1}} - Y^{0, M^{0}}} = \bar{\delta}(a) + \bar{\zeta}(1 - a) \]

    Two common decomposition

    \[ \text{TE} = \bar{\delta}(0) + \bar{\zeta}(1) \quad \text{or} \quad \text{TE} = \bar{\delta}(1) + \bar{\zeta}(0) \]

    The choice of reference level (\(a=0\) or \(a=1\)) matters when there’s an \(A \times M\) interaction (vanderweele2013mediation?).

    ImportantKey Insight

    The NDE and NIE are not symmetric. \(\bar{\delta}(0) \neq \bar{\delta}(1)\) in general, and \(\bar{\zeta}(0) \neq \bar{\zeta}(1)\). This asymmetry reflects interactions between \(A\) and \(M\) in their effect on \(Y\).

    1.4.4 Controlled Direct Effect (CDE)

    The controlled direct effect (CDE) differs from the NDE by fixing the mediator at a researcher-specified value \(m\) (not a potential value):

    \[ \text{CDE}(m) = \E{Y^{1, m} - Y^{0, m}} \]

    Interpretation: The effect of \(A\) on \(Y\) if we could intervene to set \(M = m\) for everyone. The CDE is easier to identify than the NDE (requires weaker assumptions) but less policy-relevant—we often cannot externally control \(M\) (VanderWeele, 2015).

    Critical confounding scenarios:

    1. Exposure-outcome confounders (\(C_A\)): Variables affecting both \(A\) and \(Y\) (standard confounding).
    2. Mediator-outcome confounders (\(C_M\)): Variables affecting both \(M\) and \(Y\)—even if measured pre-treatment, these require special attention.
    3. Exposure-mediator confounders: Overlap with (1) if measured before \(A\).
    4. Exposure-induced mediator-outcome confounders: A variable \(L\) affected by \(A\) that also affects both \(M\) and \(Y\)—a major threat (see VanderWeele (2015)).

    2 Clarifications of Challenging Ideas

    2.1 Why “Cross-World” Counterfactuals?

    The term “cross-world” refers to combining exposure levels from one hypothetical scenario with mediator values from another. For example, \(Y^{1, M^{0}}\) asks: “What would my outcome be if I received treatment (\(A=1\)) but my mediator took the value it would have under control (\(M^{0}\))?”

    Analogy: Imagine a clinical trial testing a new drug that works by lowering blood pressure (BP). The NIE asks: “If I gave you a placebo but magically adjusted your BP to what it would have been under the drug, how much would your heart attack risk decrease?” The NDE asks: “If I gave you the drug but kept your BP at its placebo level, how much would risk decrease?” These scenarios are impossible to create experimentally—hence “cross-world.”

    2.2 Natural vs. Controlled Effects: A Policy Perspective

    • Controlled Direct Effect (CDE): “What if we could set everyone’s mediator to \(m\)?” This is useful for understanding dose-response relationships but may be infeasible (e.g., setting everyone’s blood pressure to 120 mmHg).
    • Natural Direct Effect (NDE): “What if we changed the exposure but the mediator stayed at its natural level under control (or treatment)?” This is closer to real-world policy questions: “What’s the benefit of the drug apart from its effect on BP?”

    The NDE is more policy-relevant but harder to estimate, requiring stronger assumptions.

    2.3 Why the Mediator-Outcome Confounder Is So Dangerous

    In standard causal inference, we adjust for confounders measured before treatment. In mediation, we face a dilemma:

    • If an unmeasured variable \(U\) affects both \(M\) and \(Y\), it creates confounding (biasing the indirect effect).
    • But \(U\) is measured after \(A\) (or could be affected by \(A\)), so adjusting for it might induce post-treatment bias (opening a back-door path or creating a collider).

    Analogy: You’re trying to estimate how a job training program (\(A\)) affects income (\(Y\)) via increased motivation (\(M\)). But unobserved “natural ability” (\(U\)) affects both motivation and income. If \(U\) is unmeasured, your indirect effect estimate is confounded. If you measure ability after the training, it might itself be affected by \(A\), creating a post-treatment bias problem.

    This is why sequential ignorability (section 1.6) is so stringent.

    2.4 The Composition Assumption: Why It’s Not Trivial

    The composition assumption states:

    \[ Y_i = Y^{A_i, M^{A_i}}_i \]

    This seems obvious: “The outcome we observe is the potential outcome under the exposure and mediator values we observe.” But it has subtle implications:

    • It assumes no interference (one person’s treatment doesn’t affect another’s outcome).
    • It assumes well-defined interventions: \(A=1\) means the same thing for everyone.
    • It assumes consistency: There’s a single version of treatment (no hidden treatment variations).

    These are the same assumptions underpinning SUTVA (Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption) in standard causal inference (Rubin, 1980).

    2.5 Why Two Versions of NIE and NDE?

    Because \(\bar{\delta}(0) \neq \bar{\delta}(1)\) when there’s an \(A \times M\) interaction, we have choices. Most software (including R’s mediation package) reports both. The choice depends on the scientific question:

    • \(\bar{\delta}(0)\): Indirect effect if \(A\) were set to 0 (more common in epidemiology).
    • \(\bar{\delta}(1)\): Indirect effect if \(A\) were set to 1.

    If there’s no interaction, they’re identical.

    TipPractical Advice

    When reporting results, always specify the reference level (\(a=0\) or \(a=1\)) and explain the decomposition you’re using. Reviewers will appreciate the clarity.

    3 Simple, Hand-Calculation Numerical Example(s)

    3.1 Example: Job Training and Income

    Imagine a small study with 4 individuals. We observe:

    • \(A\): Job training (1 = received, 0 = control)
    • \(M\): Job interviews attended (count)
    • \(Y\): Income (in $1000s)
    • \(X\): Prior work experience (years)

    Observed data:

    \(i\) \(X\) \(A\) \(M\) \(Y\)
    1 2 1 5 45
    2 3 1 6 50
    3 2 0 3 35
    4 3 0 4 40

    Suppose we know (from an oracle) the complete potential outcomes for each individual:

    \(i\) \(M^{0}\) \(M^{1}\) \(Y^{0, M^{0}}\) \(Y^{0, M^{1}}\) \(Y^{1, M^{0}}\) \(Y^{1, M^{1}}\)
    1 3 5 33 37 41 45
    2 4 6 38 42 46 50
    3 3 5 35 39 43 47
    4 4 6 40 44 48 52

    Step 1: Calculate Total Effect (TE) for each individual

    \[ \tau_i = Y^{1, M^{1}}_i - Y^{0, M^{0}}_i \]

    • Individual 1: \(45 - 33 = 12\)
    • Individual 2: \(50 - 38 = 12\)
    • Individual 3: \(47 - 35 = 12\)
    • Individual 4: \(52 - 40 = 12\)

    Average TE: \(\bar{\tau} = \frac{12 + 12 + 12 + 12}{4} = 12\)

    Step 2: Calculate NIE (fixing \(A=0\))

    \[ \delta^{0}_i = Y^{0, M^{1}}_i - Y^{0, M^{0}}_i \]

    • Individual 1: \(37 - 33 = 4\)
    • Individual 2: \(42 - 38 = 4\)
    • Individual 3: \(39 - 35 = 4\)
    • Individual 4: \(44 - 40 = 4\)

    Average NIE (at \(a=0\)): \(\bar{\delta}(0) = 4\)

    Interpretation: On average, the increase in job interviews (from \(M^{0}\) to \(M^{1}\)) raises income by $4,000, even without receiving training.

    Step 3: Calculate NDE (fixing \(M\) at control level)

    \[ \zeta^{0}_i = Y^{1, M^{0}}_i - Y^{0, M^{0}}_i \]

    • Individual 1: \(41 - 33 = 8\)
    • Individual 2: \(46 - 38 = 8\)
    • Individual 3: \(43 - 35 = 8\)
    • Individual 4: \(48 - 40 = 8\)

    Average NDE (at \(a=0\)): \(\bar{\zeta}(0) = 8\)

    Interpretation: On average, the training increases income by $8,000 through pathways other than increasing job interviews (e.g., skills, confidence).

    Step 4: Verify decomposition

    \[ \bar{\tau} = \bar{\delta}(1) + \bar{\zeta}(0) \]

    Let’s compute \(\bar{\delta}(1)\):

    \[ \delta^{1}_i = Y^{1, M^{1}}_i - Y^{1, M^{0}}_i \]

    • Individual 1: \(45 - 41 = 4\)
    • Individual 2: \(50 - 46 = 4\)
    • Individual 3: \(47 - 43 = 4\)
    • Individual 4: \(52 - 48 = 4\)

    \(\bar{\delta}(1) = 4\). Now check: \(4 + 8 = 12\) ✓

    Also verify the alternative decomposition:

    \[ \bar{\tau} = \bar{\delta}(0) + \bar{\zeta}(1) \]

    Compute \(\bar{\zeta}(1)\):

    \[ \zeta^{1}_i = Y^{1, M^{1}}_i - Y^{0, M^{1}}_i \]

    • Individual 1: \(45 - 37 = 8\)
    • Individual 2: \(50 - 42 = 8\)
    • Individual 3: \(47 - 39 = 8\)
    • Individual 4: \(52 - 44 = 8\)

    \(\bar{\zeta}(1) = 8\). Check: \(4 + 8 = 12\) ✓

    Key observation: In this example, \(\bar{\delta}(0) = \bar{\delta}(1) = 4\) and \(\bar{\zeta}(0) = \bar{\zeta}(1) = 8\), indicating no \(A \times M\) interaction.

    Worked Example Summary

    • Total effect: Training increases income by $12k on average.
    • Indirect effect: $4k (33%) flows through increased job interviews.
    • Direct effect: $8k (67%) operates through other mechanisms.
    • Conclusion: The training’s primary benefit is not just more interviews—other factors (skills, network, signaling) dominate.

    3.2 Controlled Direct Effect (CDE) Calculation

    Suppose we fix \(M = 4\) interviews for everyone. The CDE compares:

    \[ \text{CDE}(4) = \E{Y^{1, 4}} - \E{Y^{0, 4}} \]

    From the table (interpolating if needed, or suppose we have):

    \(i\) \(Y^{0, 4}\) \(Y^{1, 4}\)
    1 35 43
    2 40 48
    3 37 45
    4 42 50

    \[ \text{CDE}(4) = \frac{(43 + 48 + 45 + 50)}{4} - \frac{(35 + 40 + 37 + 42)}{4} = 46.5 - 38.5 = 8 \]

    The CDE equals 8, matching \(\bar{\zeta}(0)\). This equality holds when the mediator effect is additive (no interaction).

    4.1 Drawing Mediation DAGs with ggdag

    Code 
    # Define DAG structure
mediation_dag <- dagify(
  Y ~ A + M + X,
  M ~ A + X,
  A ~ X,
  exposure = "A",
  outcome = "Y",
  coords = list(
    x = c(A = 1, M = 2, Y = 3, X = 2),

  )
)

# Plot
ggdag_status(mediation_dag) +
  theme_dag() +
  labs(title = "Mediation with Confounders X")

    Interpretation: The DAG shows \(X\) as a common cause of \(A\), \(M\), and \(Y\). To identify natural effects, we must condition on \(X\) (satisfying sequential ignorability).

    For the total effect, adjusting for \(X\) closes the backdoor path \(A \leftarrow X \to Y\).

    WarningCommon Pitfall

    For mediation effects, we need more than just blocking backdoor paths from \(A\) to \(Y\). We also need to block backdoor paths from \(M\) to \(Y\) (conditional on \(A\)). DAGs help identify these additional confounders.

    mediation_dag_u <- dagify(

  exposure = "A",
  outcome = "Y",
  latent = "U",

  )
)

ggdag_status(mediation_dag_u) +
  geom_dag_node(aes(color = ifelse(name == "U", "unmeasured", "measured"))) +

    4.4 Simulating Data Under a Known Causal Model

    We’ll generate data matching the hand-calculation example (but with more observations).

    Code 
    # Set parameters
n <- 1000

# Pre-treatment covariate
X <- rnorm(n, mean = 2.5, sd = 0.5)

# Treatment assignment (randomized conditional on X)
A <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(0.3 * (X - 2.5)))

# Mediator (affected by A and X)
M <- 3 + 2 * A + 0.5 * X + rnorm(n, 0, 0.5)

# Outcome (affected by A, M, X; no interaction for simplicity)
Y <- 30 + 8 * A + 2 * M + 2 * X + rnorm(n, 0, 2)

# Create data frame
mediation_data <- data_frame(X, A, M, Y)

head(mediation_data)

    True effects in this model:

    • Total effect: \(E[Y^{1, M^{1}} - Y^{0, M^{0}}] = 8 + 2 \times 2 = 12\) (direct effect of \(A\) plus indirect through \(M\))
    • NIE: \(E[M^{1} - M^{0}] \times \text{effect of } M = 2 \times 2 = 4\)
    • NDE: Direct effect of \(A\) = 8

    4.5 Estimating Mediation Effects with the mediation Package

  mediator = "M",
  boot = TRUE,

    Lab Retrieval Quiz

    Check Your Understanding

    Q1: What is the fundamental difference between the natural indirect effect (NIE) and the controlled direct effect (CDE)?

    Answer: The NIE measures the effect of changing the mediator from its natural value under control to its natural value under treatment (a counterfactual change). The CDE measures the effect of the exposure when the mediator is set to a fixed, researcher-chosen value (an external intervention on \(M\)). The NIE is more policy-relevant but requires stronger assumptions (sequential ignorability), while the CDE is easier to identify.

    Q2: In our lab, the proportion mediated was 42%. What does this mean in substantive terms?

    Answer: Approximately 42% of the smoking cessation intervention’s effect on lung function operates through increased exercise. The remaining 58% works through other pathways (e.g., reduced inflammation, improved cardiovascular function, or direct pharmacological effects if medication was involved).

    Q3: Why do we need to adjust for baseline covariates (\(X_1\) and \(X_2\)) in both the mediator and outcome models?

    Answer: To satisfy the two components of sequential ignorability: (1) \(X_1\) and \(X_2\) confound the relationship between \(A\) and both \(M\) and \(Y\), so we must adjust to block backdoor paths; (2) They also confound the relationship between \(M\) and \(Y\) (conditional on \(A\)), which is necessary for identifying the indirect effect.

    Q4: The sensitivity analysis showed the ACME becomes non-significant at \(\rho \approx 0.35\). What does this tell us?

    Answer: If there were unmeasured confounders of the mediator-outcome relationship strong enough to induce a correlation of 0.35 between the residuals of the mediator and outcome models, our conclusion about a positive indirect effect would be overturned. Whether this is plausible depends on domain knowledge about potential unmeasured variables.

    6 Common Pitfalls & Checks

    WarningPitfall 1: Confusing Association with Mediation

    Problem: Observing that \(M\) is correlated with both \(A\) and \(Y\) does not prove mediation. The correlation could arise from confounding.

    Check: Always draw a DAG. Identify all backdoor paths from \(M\) to \(Y\) (conditional on \(A\)). Ensure you’ve measured and adjusted for all confounders on these paths.

    WarningPitfall 2: Post-Treatment Confounding

    Problem: If a variable \(L\) is affected by \(A\) and also affects both \(M\) and \(Y\), adjusting for \(L\) creates bias (post-treatment bias), but not adjusting leaves residual confounding.

    Example: In the smoking cessation study, suppose “stress level” (\(L\)) is affected by the intervention, and stress influences both exercise (\(M\)) and lung function (\(Y\)). This violates sequential ignorability and requires advanced methods (e.g., G-computation, MSMs) (VanderWeele, 2015; vanderweele2014mediation?).

    Check: In your DAG, verify that no variables on the \(M \to Y\) path are themselves affected by \(A\). If they are, sequential ignorability fails.

    WarningPitfall 3: Measurement Error in the Mediator

    Problem: If \(M\) is measured with error, estimates of the indirect effect will be attenuated (biased toward zero) (Valeri et al., 2014).

    Check: Use validated instruments to measure \(M\). Consider multiple indicators or structural equation models with latent variables if measurement error is suspected.

    WarningPitfall 4: Ignoring Interactions

    Problem: Reporting only \(\bar{\delta}(0)\) when \(\bar{\delta}(0) \neq \bar{\delta}(1)\) can be misleading.

    Check: Always fit an outcome model with an \(A \times M\) interaction term and report both the NIE at \(A=0\) and \(A=1\). Explain which is most relevant to your research question.

    8 Appendix: Advanced Theory & Derivations

    8.1 Formal Identification Under Sequential Ignorability

    We derive the identification formula for the average natural indirect effect (NIE) under sequential ignorability (imai2010general?; pearl2012mediation?).

    Assumptions:

    1. Consistency: \(Y_i = Y^{A_i, M^{A_i}}_i\) and \(M_i = M^{A_i}_i\).
    2. Sequential Ignorability:
      • \((Y^{a', m}, M^a) \perp A \mid X\)
      • \(Y^{a', m} \perp M \mid A = a, X\)
    3. Positivity: \(0 < P(A=a \mid X) < 1\) and \(0 < P(M=m \mid A=a, X) < 1\) for all relevant \((a, m, X)\).

    Goal: Identify \(\bar{\delta}(a) = \E{Y^{a, M^{1}} - Y^{a, M^{0}}}\) in terms of observed data.

    Step 1: Expand the expectation over the covariate distribution.

    \[ \bar{\delta}(a) = \Es{X, {\E{Y^{a, M^{1}} \mid X} - \E{Y^{a, M^{0}} \mid X}} } \]

    Step 2: By sequential ignorability (part 2), \(Y^{a, m} \perp M \mid A=t, X\), so:

    \[ \E{Y^{a, m} \mid X} = \E{Y^{a, m} \mid A=t, X} \]

    By consistency, \(\E{Y^{a, m} \mid A=t, M=m, X} = \E{Y \mid A=t, M=m, X}\). Thus:

    \[ \E{Y^{a, M^{a}} \mid X} = \int \E{Y \mid A=t, M=m, X} \, dF_{M^{a} \mid X}(m \mid X) \]

    where \(F_{M^{a} \mid X}\) is the distribution of \(M^{a}\) given \(X\).

    Step 3: By sequential ignorability (part 1), \(M^{a} \perp A \mid X\), so:

    \[ F_{M^{a} \mid X}(m \mid X) = F_{M \mid A=a, X}(m \mid A=a, X) \]

    Step 4: Putting it together:

    \[ \bar{\delta}(a) = \Es{X, \left[\int \E{Y \mid A=t, M=m, X} \left( dF_{M \mid A=1, X}(m) - dF_{M \mid A=0, X}(m) \right) \right]} \]

    This is the mediation formula (pearl2012mediation?). It expresses the NIE as an integral over the observed conditional expectations weighted by the difference in mediator distributions under treatment vs. control.

    Estimation: The mediate() function implements this via:

    1. Fit \(\Ehat{M \mid A, X}\) (mediator model).
    2. Fit \(\Ehat{Y \mid A, M, X}\) (outcome model).
    3. For each observation, predict \(\hat{Y}^{a, M^{1}}\) and \(\hat{Y}^{a, M^{0}}\) by simulating \(M\) from the mediator model under \(A=1\) and \(A=0\).
    4. Average the differences.

    8.2 Relationship to the Baron-Kenny Approach

    Under a linear model with no interactions:

    • Mediator model: \(M = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 A + \alpha_2' X + \epsilon_M\)
    • Outcome model: \(Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 A + \beta_2 M + \beta_3' X + \epsilon_Y\)

    The average NIE is:

    \[ \bar{\delta}(a) = \alpha_1 \beta_2 \]

    The average NDE is:

    \[ \bar{\zeta}(a) = \beta_1 \]

    This matches the Baron-Kenny product \(a \times b = \alpha_1 \beta_2\) and direct effect \(c' = \beta_1\). However, the linear model is a special case; the counterfactual framework generalizes to nonlinear models.

    8.3 Mediation with Binary Outcomes

    When \(Y\) is binary, mediation effects are typically estimated on the difference scale (risk difference), ratio scale (relative risk), or odds ratio scale (VanderWeele & Vansteelandt, 2010).

    Example: Logistic regression models.

    • Mediator model: \(\text{logit}(P(M=1 \mid A, X)) = \alpha_0 + \alpha_1 A + \alpha_2' X\)
    • Outcome model: \(\text{logit}(P(Y=1 \mid A, M, X)) = \beta_0 + \beta_1 A + \beta_2 M + \beta_3' X\)

    The controlled direct effect on the odds ratio scale:

    \[ \text{CDE}_{\text{OR}}(m) = \exp(\beta_1) \]

    The NIE and NDE on the risk difference scale require additional integration (implemented in mediation for binary outcomes).

    Numerical illustration: Suppose \(\alpha_1 = 0.8\), \(\beta_1 = 0.5\), \(\beta_2 = 0.3\), and \(X = 0\) for simplicity.

    • Under \(A=0\): \(P(M=1 \mid A=0) = \text{expit}(\alpha_0) \approx 0.5\) (assume \(\alpha_0=0\)).
    • Under \(A=1\)
    • Under \(A=1\): \(P(M=1 \mid A=1) = \text{expit}(\alpha_0 + 0.8) \approx 0.69\).

    Outcome probabilities:

    • \(P(Y=1 \mid A=0, M=0) = \text{expit}(\beta_0) \approx 0.5\) (assume \(\beta_0=0\)).
    • \(P(Y=1 \mid A=0, M=1) = \text{expit}(\beta_0 + 0.3) \approx 0.57\).
    • \(P(Y=1 \mid A=1, M=0) = \text{expit}(\beta_0 + 0.5) \approx 0.62\).
    • \(P(Y=1 \mid A=1, M=1) = \text{expit}(\beta_0 + 0.5 + 0.3) \approx 0.69\).

    NIE calculation (at \(A=0\)):

    \[ \bar{\delta}(0) = E[Y^{0, M^{1}}] - E[Y^{0, M^{0}}] \]

    \[ = P(M=1 \mid A=1) \cdot P(Y=1 \mid A=0, M=1) + P(M=0 \mid A=1) \cdot P(Y=1 \mid A=0, M=0) \]

    \[ - \left[ P(M=1 \mid A=0) \cdot P(Y=1 \mid A=0, M=1) + P(M=0 \mid A=0) \cdot P(Y=1 \mid A=0, M=0) \right] \]

    \[ = 0.69 \times 0.57 + 0.31 \times 0.50 - (0.50 \times 0.57 + 0.50 \times 0.50) \]

    \[ = 0.548 - 0.535 = 0.013 \]

    The NIE on the risk difference scale is approximately 1.3 percentage points. This modest effect reflects the nonlinearity of the logistic function.

    8.4 The G-Formula for Mediation

    The G-formula (or standardization formula) provides a nonparametric identification strategy (Hernán & Robins, 2020; Robins, 1986). For the NIE:

    \[ \bar{\delta}(a) = \sum_{x, m} E[Y \mid A=t, M=m, X=x] \left[ P(M=m \mid A=1, X=x) - P(M=m \mid A=0, X=x) \right] P(X=x) \]

    This formula marginalizes over the confounder distribution, making it appropriate for complex confounding structures.

    Implementation: The mediation package uses Monte Carlo integration to approximate this formula when continuous mediators or covariates are present.

    8.5 Mediation with Time-Varying Treatments

    When treatments vary over time (e.g., \(A_1, A_2, \ldots\)), mediation becomes more complex. Marginal structural models (MSMs) combined with inverse probability weighting can estimate longitudinal mediation effects (VanderWeele & Eric J. Tchetgen Tchetgen, 2017; vanderweele2014mediation?).

    Example: A weight loss intervention at baseline (\(A_1\)) and 6 months (\(A_2\)), with diet adherence (\(M_1, M_2\)) as time-varying mediators.

    The controlled direct effect of \((A_1, A_2)\) on weight loss \(Y\) at 12 months, fixing \((M_1, M_2)\) to \((m_1, m_2)\):

    \[ \text{CDE}(m_1, m_2) = E[Y^{a_1=1, a_2=1, m_1, m_2}] - E[Y^{a_1=0, a_2=0, m_1, m_2}] \]

    Natural effects in this context require defining “natural” mediator values under different treatment histories—a conceptually challenging task (VanderWeele & Eric J. Tchetgen Tchetgen, 2017).

    8.6 Instrumental Variable (IV) Approaches to Mediation

    When sequential ignorability is violated (unmeasured mediator-outcome confounding), an instrumental variable for the mediator can sometimes identify mediation effects (dunn2004estimating?; small2012mediation?).

    Setup: Let \(Z\) be an instrument for \(M\) such that:

    1. \(Z\) affects \(M\) (relevance).
    2. \(Z\) affects \(Y\) only through \(M\) (exclusion restriction).
    3. \(Z\) is independent of unmeasured confounders of \(M \to Y\).

    Under these assumptions, the local average treatment effect (LATE) framework can bound or point-identify the indirect effect in subpopulations (compliers).

    Example: In a trial of a behavioral intervention (\(A\)), suppose treatment encouragement (\(Z\)) serves as an instrument for intervention uptake (\(M\)).

    Estimation: Two-stage least squares (2SLS) or more sophisticated approaches like principal stratification (frangakis2002principal?).

    Numerical Illustration:

    Suppose:

    • \(Z \sim \text{Bernoulli}(0.5)\) (randomized encouragement)
    • \(M = 0.2 + 0.6 Z + U_M\), where \(U_M \sim N(0, 0.3)\)
    • \(Y = 1 + 0.8 M + U_Y\), where \(U_Y = 0.5 U_M + \epsilon_Y\) (confounded by \(U_M\))

    Standard mediation analysis would be biased due to \(U_M\) confounding. Using IV:

    • First stage: Regress \(M\) on \(Z\) → \(\hat{\beta}_Z = 0.6\)
    • Reduced form: Regress \(Y\) on \(Z\) → \(\hat{\gamma}_Z = 0.6 \times 0.8 = 0.48\)
    • IV estimate of effect of \(M\) on \(Y\): \(\hat{\beta}_M^{IV} = \frac{0.48}{0.6} = 0.8\)

    This recovers the true effect of \(M\) on \(Y\) without bias from \(U_M\).

    8.7 Bounds and Sensitivity Analysis

    When sequential ignorability fails, mediation effects may not be point-identified, but bounds can sometimes be derived (Manski, 1990; imai2010general?).

    Sharp bounds (no parametric assumptions):

    \[ \max \left\{ 0, \bar{\tau} - \bar{\zeta}_{\text{upper}} \right\} \leq \bar{\delta} \leq \min \left\{ \bar{\tau}, \bar{\delta}_{\text{upper}} \right\} \]

    where \(\bar{\zeta}_{\text{upper}}\) and \(\bar{\delta}_{\text{upper}}\) are derived from the observed data under worst-case confounding scenarios.

    Parametric sensitivity analysis (imai2010general?):

    Model the unmeasured confounder as inducing a correlation \(\rho\) between residuals \(\epsilon_M\) and \(\epsilon_Y\):

    \[ \begin{bmatrix} \epsilon_M \\ \epsilon_Y \end{bmatrix} \sim N \left( \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix}, \begin{bmatrix} \sigma_M^2 & \rho \sigma_M \sigma_Y \\ \rho \sigma_M \sigma_Y & \sigma_Y^2 \end{bmatrix} \right) \]

    The medsens() function varies \(\rho\) and re-estimates mediation effects, producing a sensitivity plot.

    Numerical Illustration:

    Suppose the observed ACME is 5 with \(\rho = 0\) (no confounding). If \(\rho = 0.3\), the ACME might shrink to 3. If \(\rho = 0.5\), it might become non-significant (crossing zero).

    Interpretation: The “tipping point” value of \(\rho\) at which conclusions change informs robustness. Researchers compare this to plausible confounding scenarios in their domain.

    8.8 Mediation in Structural Equation Models (SEM)

    Structural Equation Modeling (SEM) provides a flexible framework for mediation with multiple mediators, latent variables, and complex error structures (bollen1989structural?; muthén2015regression?).

    Advantages:

    • Can handle measurement error via latent variables.
    • Simultaneous estimation of multiple pathways.
    • Model fit indices assess overall model adequacy.

    Limitations:

    • Relies heavily on distributional assumptions (multivariate normality).
    • Model fit does not guarantee causal validity (requires correct causal structure).

    Example SEM Path Model:

    Total indirect effect: \(\alpha_1 \beta_1 + \alpha_2 \beta_2\)

    Direct effect: \(\gamma\)

    R Implementation (using lavaan package):

    Code 
    library(lavaan)

# Define SEM model
sem_model <- '
  # Mediator equations
  M1 ~ alpha1*A
  M2 ~ alpha2*A
  
  # Outcome equation
  Y ~ beta1*M1 + beta2*M2 + gamma*A
  
  # Covariances
  M1 ~~ M2
  
  # Define indirect effects
  indirect1 := alpha1 * beta1
  indirect2 := alpha2 * beta2
  total_indirect := indirect1 + indirect2
  
  # Define direct effect
  direct := gamma
  
  # Total effect
  total := direct + total_indirect
'

# Fit model
sem_fit <- sem(sem_model, data = mydata)

# View results
summary(sem_fit, standardized = TRUE, rsquare = TRUE)

    8.9 Causal Mediation with Survival Outcomes

    When the outcome is time-to-event (survival), mediation analysis requires special attention to censoring and time-varying confounding (lange2011assessment?; vanderweele2011causal?).

    Challenges:

    • The mediator may itself be affected by early outcomes (semi-competing risks).
    • Time-varying confounders affected by treatment can induce bias.
    • Censoring must be handled appropriately.

    Approach: Use counterfactual survival functions \(S^{a, m}(a) = P(T^{a, m} > a)\), where \(T\) is survival time.

    NIE for survival:

    \[ \text{NIE}(a) = S^{0, M^{1}}(a) - S^{0, M^{0}}(a) \]

    This measures the change in survival probability at time \(a\) due to the mediator shift.

    Estimation: Parametric (e.g., Weibull regression for mediator and outcome) or semiparametric (Cox models with causal imputation) approaches are available (tchetgen2011causal?).

    8.10 Efficiency Bounds and Optimal Estimation

    Under sequential ignorability, mediation effects are nonparametrically identified, but estimation efficiency depends on the chosen estimation strategy (tchetgen2012semiparametric?; zheng2012targeted?).

    Semiparametric efficiency theory establishes the efficient influence function (EIF) for the NIE. Estimators achieving the semiparametric efficiency bound (minimum variance among all regular estimators) are called efficient.

    Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (TMLE) and doubly robust estimation achieve this bound (zheng2012targeted?):

    • Doubly robust: Consistent if either the mediator model or the outcome model (but not necessarily both) is correctly specified.
    • TMLE: Updates initial estimates using the EIF to achieve efficiency.

    Example EIF for NIE (simplified):

    \[ \phi_{\delta}(O) = \frac{A}{\pi(X)} \left[ Y - E[Y \mid A=1, M, X] \right] + E[Y \mid A=1, M^1, X] - E[Y \mid A=1, M^0, X] - \bar{\delta} \]

    where \(O = (X, A, M, Y)\) and \(\pi(X) = P(A=1 \mid X)\).

    Numerical comparison:

    Suppose the true NIE is 5. Under misspecification:

    • Correctly specified mediator model, misspecified outcome model: Estimator A gives \(\hat{\delta} = 5.2 \pm 0.8\).
    • Misspecified mediator model, correctly specified outcome model: Estimator A gives \(\hat{\delta} = 4.9 \pm 0.9\).
    • Doubly robust estimator: \(\hat{\delta} = 5.0 \pm 0.7\) (consistent under either model being correct).
    • TMLE: \(\hat{\delta} = 5.0 \pm 0.65\) (efficient, narrowest CI).

    The efficiency gain from TMLE is modest but can be important in small samples.
