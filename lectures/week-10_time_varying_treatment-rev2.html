Learning Objectives

By the end of this module, you will be able to:

  • Explain treatment–confounder feedback and why standard adjustment fails (Hernán & Robins, What If, §19–20).
  • State and justify the three core identification assumptions—sequential exchangeability, positivity, and consistency—and diagnose threats to each.
  • Derive and compute stabilized inverse probability weights (IPWs) for multi-time treatments (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000).
  • Estimate parameters of a marginal structural model (MSM) with weighted GEE/GLM and interpret them as effects of sustained strategies.
  • Compare and contrast MSM/IPW with the g-formula and structural nested models; articulate when each is advantageous (Neal, Intro to CI, Ch. 10–11).
  • Evaluate diagnostics (weight distribution, truncation, covariate balance) and implement sensitivity checks.

1 Comprehensive Topic Explanation

1.1 The Longitudinal Setup

We observe for each individual \(i\) across \(k=0,\dots,K\):

  • Time-varying covariates \(\bar L_k=(L_0,\dots,L_k)\),
  • Treatments \(\bar A_k=(A_0,\dots,A_k)\),
  • Outcome \(Y\) at \(K{+}1\).

Treatment–confounder feedback: \(L_k\) both (i) confounds \(A_k \to Y\) and (ii) lies on the pathway \(A_{k-1}\to L_k \to Y\). Adjusting for \(L_k\) in a standard outcome regression blocks a piece of the very effect we want (mediated path), inducing bias or sign reversal (Hernán & Robins §19).

1.2 Target Estimands & MSMs

We shift from “effect of \(A_k\) given \(L_k\)” to the marginal effect of a sustained strategy, e.g., “treat whenever biomarker \(> x\).” MSMs parameterize the marginal counterfactual mean under a regime:

\[ \mathbb{E}\{Y^{\bar a}\} = m(\bar a; \beta), \]

e.g., a logit-linear MSM for two time points:

\[ \text{logit}\{\mathbb{E}(Y^{a_0,a_1})\}=\beta_0+\beta_1 a_0+\beta_2 a_1+\beta_3 a_0 a_1. \]

Parameters \(\beta\) are interpreted as causal contrasts of sustained histories.

1.3 Identification Assumptions (Hernán & Robins §19; Neal Ch. 10)

  • Sequential exchangeability: \(Y^{\bar a}\ \indep\ A_k \mid \bar L_k,\bar A_{k-1}\) for all \(k\). Given observed history, treatment is as-if random.
  • Positivity: \(\Pr(A_k=a_k\mid \bar L_k=\bar \ell_k,\bar A_{k-1}=\bar a_{k-1})>0\) whenever \(\Pr(\bar L_k=\bar \ell_k,\bar A_{k-1}=\bar a_{k-1})>0\).
  • Consistency: If an individual follows \(\bar A=\bar a\), then \(Y=Y^{\bar a}\).

1.4 IPW Construction & Stabilized Weights

To break confounding by \(\bar L_k\), define stabilized weight for individual \(i\):

\[ SW_i=\prod_{k=0}^{K}\frac{\Pr(A_{ik}=a_{ik}\mid \bar A_{i,k-1})} {\Pr(A_{ik}=a_{ik}\mid \bar A_{i,k-1},\bar L_{ik})}. \]

Intuition: upweight histories that were unlikely to get the treatment they received given their covariates; downweight those that were likely. In the weighted pseudo-population, \(\bar L_k \not\to A_k\) by design; MSM parameters can be fit by weighted regression/GEEs (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000).

1.5 Estimation Strategies

  • IPTW of the marginal mean: estimate \(\mathbb{E}(Y^{\bar a})\) by weighting each unit by the inverse probability of their observed treatment path (restricted to those following \(\bar a\) when estimating that regime).
  • Weighted GLM/GEE (common in practice): regress \(Y\) on functions of \(\bar A\) per the MSM using \(SW\) as weights; use robust (sandwich) SEs.
  • Variance: robust sandwich; cluster on ID for repeated measures.
  • Censoring: incorporate censoring weights by multiplying treatment weights with inverse probabilities of remaining uncensored (Hernán & Robins §12, §19).

1.6 MSMs Vs G-formula Vs SNMs (compare–contrast)

  • MSM + IPW: Targets marginal regime effects; robust to outcome model misspecification but sensitive to treatment model/positivity & weight instability.
  • G-formula (parametric g-comp): Models \(Y \mid\) history and simulates under regimes. Sensitive to outcome model misspecification; no unstable weights but more modeling.
  • SNMs/g-estimation: Alternative parameterization focusing on treatment blip effects; useful with time-varying treatments and confounding; often less familiar operationally.
Decision Guide

When choose what? - Strong domain knowledge to specify \(Y\mid\) history → g-formula. - Cleaner treatment model & desire marginal interpretation → MSM. - Focus on dynamic blip functions & structural nesting → SNM.

Citations: Hernán & Robins §12, §19–20; Neal Ch. 10–11; Robins (1986, 1997, 2000); VanderWeele Ch. 19.

2 Intuitive Clarifications of Challenging Ideas

Learning Tip

Think of IPW as “re-queuing the study” so that—conditional on past treatments—the next treatment looks like a coin flip, independent of the covariates that used to influence it.

Visual metaphor: - Pipes and valves. Past treatment opens/closes valves that change \(L_k\) (flow rate). Those \(L_k\) then steer future treatment. IPW temporarily “disconnects” the \(L_k \to A_k\) pipe so we can see the net effect of opening the treatment valves over time.

Common misconceptions (and fixes): - “I adjusted for \(L_k\) in an outcome regression, so I controlled confounding.” No—you also block mediated effects of earlier \(A\) through \(L_k\), biasing the target marginal effect (Hernán & Robins §19). - “Large weights just mean more precision.” No—they usually signal near-positivity violations; consider truncation, coarsening, or redefining the target regime/support. - “Stabilized weights always fix everything.” They reduce variance, not bias from misspecified models or violations of assumptions.

DAG for treatment–confounder feedback (drawn below) clarifies the double role of \(L_1\).

3 Simple, Hand-Calculation Numerical Example(s)

3.1 Four-person Toy Example (IPW mechanics)

Two times (\(k=0,1\)), all binary. Data:

ID L0 A0 L1 A1 Y
1 0 0 0 0 0
2 0 1 1 1 1
3 1 0 1 0 0
4 1 1 0 1 1

Step A (fit by hand): empirical treatment probabilities

  • \(P(A_0{=}1\mid L_0{=}0)=1/2\), \(P(A_0{=}1\mid L_0{=}1)=1/2\)
  • \(P(A_1{=}1\mid A_0{=}1, L_1{=}0)=1\), \(P(A_1{=}1\mid A_0{=}0,L_1{=}0)=0\), \(P(A_1{=}1\mid A_0{=}1,L_1{=}1)=1\), \(P(A_1{=}1\mid A_0{=}0,L_1{=}1)=0\) (from table)

Numerators (stabilized) use reduced models: - \(P(A_0{=}1)=1/2\); \(P(A_1{=}1\mid A_0)\) equals 1 when \(A_0{=}1\), and 0 when \(A_0{=}0\) (in this tiny table).

Step B (compute each \(SW_i\)): Example, ID 2 has \((A_0,A_1)=(1,1)\) with \((L_0,L_1)=(0,1)\):

\[ SW_2=\frac{P(A_0{=}1)}{P(A_0{=}1\mid L_0{=}0)}\cdot \frac{P(A_1{=}1\mid A_0{=}1)}{P(A_1{=}1\mid A_0{=}1, L_1{=}1)} = \frac{1/2}{1/2}\cdot\frac{1}{1}=1. \]

Do likewise for IDs 1,3,4 (here, all weights turn out 1 due to deterministic \(A_1\) within strata). This illustrates stabilized weight definition even if trivial.

Step C (weighted mean under regime “always treat”) To estimate \(\mathbb{E}(Y^{1,1})\), keep only those with \((A_0,A_1)=(1,1)\) (IDs 2 and 4), and compute the weighted mean of \(Y\): \((1\cdot1 + 1\cdot1)/(1+1)=1\). For “never treat,” use \((0,0)\) (ID 1 only): mean \(=0\) (ID 3 has \((0,0)\)? No; it’s \((0,0)\) at \(A\), yes—\(Y=0\)). Estimated effect (risk difference) \(\approx 1-0=1\) in this toy.

📌 Key Assumption

This hand example has deterministic \(A_1\) given \((A_0, L_1)\), making positivity fragile. Real data rarely behave so cleanly—expect non-trivial weights and perform diagnostics.

3.2 Scaffolding Extension (non-trivial numerics)

Modify the table so \(A_1\) sometimes flips in \((A_0{=}1,L_1{=}0)\) with probability \(0.7\) and in \((A_0{=}1,L_1{=}1)\) with \(0.6\). Recompute denominators, numerators (e.g., \(P(A_1{=}1\mid A_0{=}1)=0.65\)), then each \(SW_i\). Observe how rare patterns get larger weights.

4.2 Basic Simulation: Two time Points, Binary Outcome

Code 
set.seed(123)
n <- 4000
L0 <- rbinom(n, 1, 0.5)
A0 <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.5 + 1.0 * L0))
L1 <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.2 + 1.2 * A0 + 0.6 * L0))
A1 <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(-0.8 + 1.6 * L1 + 0.3 * A0))
linY <- -0.3 + 0.9 * A0 + 1.1 * A1 + 0.5 * L1
Y <- rbinom(n, 1, plogis(linY))
dat <- tibble(id = 1:n, L0, A0, L1, A1, Y)

# Denominator Models (rich)
pA0_den <- glm(A0 ~ L0, family = binomial, data = dat)$fitted.values
pA1_den <- glm(A1 ~ A0 + L0 + L1, family = binomial, data = dat)$fitted.values

# Numerator Models (reduced)
pA0_num <- glm(A0 ~ 1, family = binomial, data = dat)$fitted.values
pA1_num <- glm(A1 ~ A0, family = binomial, data = dat)$fitted.values

dat <- dat |> mutate(sw = (pA0_num / pA0_den) * (pA1_num / pA1_den))

ggplot(dat, aes(sw)) +
  geom_histogram(bins = 50)

Interpretation. A tight weight distribution suggests OK overlap; heavy tails signal potential positivity issues.

Optional truncation & normalization:

Code 
alpha <- 0.01
lower <- quantile(dat$sw, alpha)
upper <- quantile(dat$sw, 1 - alpha)
dat <- dat |>
  mutate(
    sw_trunc = pmin(pmax(sw, lower), upper),
    sw_norm = sw / mean(sw),
    swt_norm = sw_trunc / mean(sw_trunc)
  )
summary(dat$sw)

Code 
# MSM with Weighted GEE (independence Working correlation)
msm <- geeglm(

  data = dat,

Compare–contrast: The naïve model adjusts for \(L_1\) (a mediator of \(A_0\)), biasing \(\hat\beta_{A0}\). The MSM targets marginal effects; coefficients approximate the causal log-odds effects of treatment at each time.

4.5 Advanced Simulation: Add Censoring & Censored Weights

Code 
set.seed(1)
dat2 <- dat |>
  mutate(C1 = rbinom(n(), 1, plogis(-2 + 1.3 * L1 - 0.6 * A1))) # 1 = censored
# Denominator and Numerator Models for Censoring (prob of *not* censored)
pC1_den <- glm(
  I(1 - C1) ~ A0 + A1 + L0 + L1,
  family = binomial,
  data = dat2
)$fitted.values
pC1_num <- glm(
  I(1 - C1) ~ A0 + A1,
  family = binomial,
  data = dat2
)$fitted.values
dat2 <- dat2 |>
  mutate(
    sc = (pC1_num / pC1_den),
    swc = sw * sc,
    swc_trunc = pmin(pmax(swc, lower), upper)
  )

# Fit MSM among Uncensored with Combined Weights
msm_c <- geeglm(
  Y ~ A0 + A1,
  id = id,
  weights = swc_trunc,
  data = filter(dat2, C1 == 0),
  family = binomial,
  corstr = "independence"
)
summary(msm_c)

Note. For time-to-event outcomes, use survival::coxph with weights (and robust variance).

  • In your own study area, propose a sustained strategy (static or dynamic). Which covariates risk violating positivity, and how would you mitigate that risk?

6 Common Pitfalls & Checks

Common Pitfall

Extreme or unstable weights (heavy right tail) → inflated variance and sensitivity. Check: histograms, quantiles; report \(\max(sw)\) and \(\mathrm{CV}(sw)\). Mitigate: truncation (e.g., at 1st/99th), coarsen covariates, redefine estimand (narrow regime or support), or switch to g-formula if overlap is non-existent (Hernán & Robins §19–20).

Common Pitfall

Model misspecification in treatment models → biased MSM even with perfect outcome model neutrality. Check: include flexible terms (splines, interactions), consider Super Learner (if allowed), compare alternative specifications (Neal Ch. 10).

Common Pitfall

Adjusting for mediators in the outcome model (e.g., adding \(L_1\)) undermines the marginal target. Check: keep MSM outcome models marginal in \(\bar A\) and push confounding control into weights.

Check Your Understanding

Why might you prefer the g-formula over MSMs when weight tails remain heavy even after truncation?

7 Advanced Teaching Activities & Interactivity

  • Think–Pair–Share: Given a clinical p1olicy “treat if \(L_k > c\),” decide if it’s static or dynamic; identify confounders and mediators at each time; sketch the DAG.
  • Problem-based case: Simulate a cohort with near-deterministic treatment when \(L_1\) is extreme. Attempt MSM, then redesign the target estimand to satisfy positivity (e.g., restrict to \(L_1\in[0.2,0.8]\)).
  • Spaced/interleaved quiz: Alternate items asking for (i) weight formulas and (ii) g-formula simulation steps.
  • Self-explanation: Students write a paragraph comparing the interpretation of \(\beta_{A1}\) in MSM vs the coefficient of \(A_1\) in a fully adjusted outcome regression.
  • Optional Shiny idea: Live sliders for \(A_1\) assignment strength and \(L_1\) effect; show changes in weight tails and MSM bias.

8 Appendix: Advanced Theory & Derivations (optional)

A.1 Identification Sketch (two times)

Under sequential exchangeability, positivity, and consistency,

\[ \mathbb{E}(Y^{a_0,a_1}) = \mathbb{E}\bigg[\frac{\mathbb{I}(A_0{=}a_0)\mathbb{I}(A_1{=}a_1)} {\Pr(A_0{=}a_0\mid L_0)\Pr(A_1{=}a_1\mid A_0,L_0,L_1)}Y\bigg]. \]

Replacing denominators with estimated propensities yields IPTW. Stabilized weights multiply by numerators \(\Pr(A_0{=}a_0)\) and \(\Pr(A_1{=}a_1\mid A_0)\) to improve efficiency (Robins, Hernán & Brumback, 2000; Hernán & Robins §19).

A.2 Efficiency and variance

Weighted estimating equations with independence working correlation yield consistent estimates of MSM parameters; robust sandwich variance accounts for misspecification of the working correlation (Liang & Zeger, 1986; see also Hernán & Robins §20).

A.3 Dynamic Regimes

For a dynamic rule \(d=(d_0,d_1)\) with \(a_k=d_k(\bar L_k,\bar A_{k-1})\), replace indicators \(\mathbb{I}(A_k{=}a_k)\) with \(\mathbb{I}(A_k{=}d_k(\cdot))\) in the IPTW estimator; the MSM can include summaries of “following the rule” (Cunningham §10; VanderWeele §19).

A.4 Numerical Illustration (mini)

Suppose \(\Pr(A_0{=}1\mid L_0)=\text{logit}^{-1}(-0.5+L_0)\); draw 10 individuals; compute denominators and numerators, then \(SW_i\). Summarize \(\bar{SW}\), \(\max(SW)\), and the truncated proportion. Observe how increasing the \(L_1 \to A_1\) coefficient inflates \(\max(SW)\).
