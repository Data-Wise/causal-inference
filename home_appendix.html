STAT 579 Final Project: The Target Trial Challenge

Welcome to the capstone experience for STAT 579! This project is where theory becomes practice. You will move beyond textbook exercises to tackle a real-world causal question from start to finish, applying the rigorous principles of modern causal inference to a topic that matters to you.

The challenge? To design and emulate a perfect experiment—a target trial—that you wish had been conducted, using the messy, observational data we so often have in the real world.

The Guiding Philosophy: Think Like a Trialist

At the heart of this project is the Target Trial Emulation framework. Why? Because it provides a powerful and structured way to approach causal inference with observational data.

Imagine you are a detective arriving at a crime scene. You cannot rewind time to see what happened, but you can use the evidence available to reconstruct the most likely sequence of events.

Similarly, we cannot go back in time to run the perfect randomized trial. Instead, we act as “data detectives.” We start by carefully designing the protocol for the ideal experiment we wish we could have run. Then, we use our observational data and causal methods to reconstruct, or emulate, that trial as closely as possible. This disciplined process illuminates our assumptions and strengthens our causal claims.

Your Research Journey: From Question to Conclusion 🗺️

This project will unfold over the semester, with milestones designed to keep you on track. Your journey will follow four key stages:

  1. Formulate a Precise Causal Question (Weeks 1-3): It all starts here. You will identify a compelling causal question and use a DAG to map out your assumptions about the world. What effect do you want to measure? What are the key players (confounders, mediators)?
  2. Design the Ideal Trial (Weeks 4-8): You will write the “protocol” for your hypothetical target trial. Who would be eligible? What are the exact treatment strategies being compared? How would you measure the outcome? This blueprint is your North Star.
  3. Emulate the Trial & Estimate the Effect (Weeks 9-12): This is where you connect your ideal protocol to your chosen dataset. You will apply one or more of the sophisticated methods from our course—like propensity score weighting or g-computation—to estimate the causal effect, carefully checking that your assumptions (like positivity) hold.
  4. Assess Robustness & Interpret (Weeks 13-14): A causal claim is only as strong as its assumptions. You will conduct sensitivity analyses (e.g., calculating an E-value) to see how fragile your results are to potential unmeasured confounding. Finally, you will present your findings and their real-world implications.

Skills You Will Master

By completing this project, you will build and demonstrate a portfolio of in-demand skills:

  • Causal Reasoning: Translating a real-world problem into a formal causal question with a supporting DAG.
  • Study Design: Specifying and emulating a target trial protocol.
  • Modern Estimation: Implementing methods like IPW, matching, or g-computation in R.
  • Assumption Diagnostics: Assessing positivity, covariate balance, and model fit.
  • Robustness Checking: Quantifying the potential impact of unmeasured confounding through sensitivity analysis.
  • Scientific Communication: Presenting your causal findings clearly and honestly in both written and oral formats.

🎓 Roles & Responsibilities

🧑‍🏫 Discussion Leader

  1. Submit Paper Choice: Email your selected paper to the instructor by Thursday of the week prior to your assigned week.
  2. Post Discussion Starter: Post your discussion starter in the weekly Canvas thread by Sunday at 11:59 pm. It must include:
    • A ~300-word summary of the paper.
    • Your answers to the three guiding questions from the Weekly Readings Schedule.
    • A key takeaway from the paper.
  3. Lead In-Class Discussion: Be prepared to lead the in-class discussion on Tuesday.

✍️ All Other Students

  1. Read: Read the paper selected by the Discussion Leader.
  2. Post Critique: Post your critique in the weekly Canvas thread by Monday at 11:59 pm. It must include:
    • A brief summary of the paper.
    • Your answers to the three guiding questions from the Weekly Readings Schedule.
    • A personal takeaway from the paper.
