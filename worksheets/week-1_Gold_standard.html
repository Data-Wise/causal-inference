Hello class. This worksheet is designed to give you hands-on practice with the concepts we discussed in Lecture 1. The goal is not just to get the right answer, but to understand the process of thinking causally.

Background / Scenario

A hospital has conducted a study comparing two treatments for kidney stones:

  • Treatment A: A new, advanced surgical procedure.

  • Treatment B: The standard, less invasive treatment.

The hospital has provided you with data on 400 patients who received one of the two treatments. The outcome of interest is whether the treatment was a success (i.e., the kidney stones were completely removed).

Here is the raw data:

Treatment A (New) Treatment B (Standard) Total
Success 170 150 320
Failure 30 50 80
Total 200 200 400

Learning Objective

By the end of this worksheet, you will be able to:

  1. Calculate and compare crude (overall) rates of success.

  2. Identify a potential confounding variable from a problem description.

  3. Perform a stratified analysis to calculate group-specific rates.

  4. Explain how confounding can lead to Simpson’s Paradox, where group-specific trends are reversed in the aggregate data.

  5. Draw a simple Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) to represent the relationships between variables.

Step-by-Step Scaffolding Tasks

Step 1: The Naive (Overall) Comparison

First, let’s look at the data from a purely associational perspective, ignoring any other factors.

  • Task 1a: Calculate the overall success rate for patients who received Treatment A.

    • Guiding Question: What is the total number of patients who had a success with Treatment A? What is the total number of patients who received Treatment A?

    • Calculation: (Number of Successes for A) / (Total Number receiving A) = ?

  • Task 1b: Calculate the overall success rate for patients who received Treatment B.

    • Calculation: (Number of Successes for B) / (Total Number receiving B) = ?

  • Task 1c: Based only on these overall rates, which treatment appears to be more effective?

Step 2: Identifying a Potential Confounder

In our lecture, we discussed that observational comparisons can be misleading. A key piece of information was left out of the table above: doctors tend to assign the more invasive Treatment A to patients with large kidney stones, while patients with small stones often receive the standard Treatment B. Stone size is also known to affect the difficulty of the treatment.

  • Task 2a: In this scenario, what is the third variable that might be related to both the treatment assigned and the treatment’s success? This is our potential confounder.

  • Task 2b: Explain why this variable could be a confounder.

    • Guiding Question: How might stone size influence a doctor’s choice of treatment? How might stone size independently influence whether a treatment is successful?

Step 3: The Stratified Analysis (Controlling for the Confounder)

Now, let’s re-examine the data, but this time we will “stratify” it by stone size. This is like looking at the data for small stones and large stones separately.

Here is the complete data, broken down by stone size:

Group 1: Patients with Small Stones

Treatment A Treatment B
Success 81 135
Failure 9 15
Total 90 150

Group 2: Patients with Large Stones

Treatment A Treatment B
Success 89 15
Failure 21 35
Total 110 50

  • Task 3a: Calculate the success rate for each treatment only for patients with small stones.

    • Success Rate for A (Small Stones): ? / ? = ?

    • Success Rate for B (Small Stones): ? / ? = ?

    • Which treatment is better for this group?

  • Task 3b: Calculate the success rate for each treatment only for patients with large stones.

    • Success Rate for A (Large Stones): ? / ? = ?

    • Success Rate for B (Large Stones): ? / ? = ?

    • Which treatment is better for this group?

Step 4: Visualizing the Causal Structure (DAG)

Let’s draw a map of our causal assumptions.

  • Task 4: Create a Directed Acyclic Graph (DAG) with three nodes: Treatment Choice, Stone Size, and Success. Draw arrows to represent the causal relationships you uncovered in the steps above.

    • Guiding Questions:

      1. Does the treatment choice cause a successful outcome? (This is the arrow we hope to understand). Draw it.

      2. Does the patient’s stone size cause the doctor to choose a particular treatment? Draw it.

      3. Does the stone size cause a treatment to be more or less successful, regardless of which treatment is used? Draw it.

Reflection / Discussion Questions

  1. Compare your answer from Task 1c with your answers from Task 3a and Task 3b. What do you notice? This reversal is known as Simpson’s Paradox.

  2. Explain, in your own words, why Treatment A looked worse in the overall analysis but better in both the small-stone and large-stone groups. (Hint: Think about which group of patients was disproportionately receiving Treatment A).

  3. What does this exercise teach you about the danger of drawing causal conclusions from simple, overall comparisons of two groups in observational data?

Optional Challenge

Imagine a different variable: Post-operative Care Unit. Suppose all patients who get Treatment A are sent to ICU Care, while patients getting Treatment B are sent to Standard Care. A researcher decides to “control” for this by stratifying the analysis by care unit. Why might this be a mistake? Think about the timing. Can the choice of care unit be a common cause of both treatment and success, or is it something else? (This touches on the concept of a mediator, which we will discuss later in the course).
