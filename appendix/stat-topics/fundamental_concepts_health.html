Welcome. Let’s begin our exploration of the fundamental concepts that form the bedrock of causal inference. Before we can navigate the complexities of methods like IP weighting or g-estimation, we must have a rock-solid understanding of the basic epidemiological measures used to quantify associations and, under specific assumptions, causal effects.

Think of these concepts as the essential vocabulary of our field. Mastering them will allow you to precisely state your causal question, understand the measures reported in the scientific literature, and correctly interpret the output of your own analyses. We will draw heavily from Chapters 1 and 17 of Hernán and Robins (HR) to build this foundation.

Let’s start with the most critical distinction of all.

The Core Distinction: Association vs. Causation

👨‍🏫 Intuitive Understanding:

At its heart, epidemiology is often concerned with figuring out if an exposure (like a medication, a lifestyle choice, or an environmental factor) causes an outcome (like a disease, recovery, or death).

An association is simply an observed statistical relationship. We observe that people with the exposure are more or less likely to have the outcome than people without the exposure. For example, we might observe that individuals who carry a lighter in their pocket are more likely to develop lung cancer than those who do not. This is a real, observable association.

A causal effect, however, is a much stronger claim. It means that the exposure itself influences the outcome. For the lighter example, we intuitively know the lighter doesn’t cause cancer. The association exists because a third factor, smoking, is a common cause of both carrying a lighter and developing lung cancer.

This simple example illustrates the classic adage: “Association is not causation.”

Our central task in this course is to determine the conditions under which an observed association can be interpreted as a causal effect. To do this, we need a formal language.

Mathematical Foundations

The language we use is that of potential outcomes (or counterfactuals), as introduced in HR Chapter 1[cite: 38]. For an individual \(i\), and a binary exposure \(A\) (1=exposed, 0=unexposed), we define two potential outcomes:

  • \(Y_i^{a=1}\): The outcome for individual \(i\) if they were exposed.
  • \(Y_i^{a=0}\): The outcome for individual \(i\) if they were unexposed.

Causation is about comparing these potential outcomes for the same individuals. A causal effect exists for an individual if \(Y_i^{a=1} \neq Y_i^{a=0}\)[cite: 38]. Since we can’t observe both potential outcomes for the same person at the same time (this is the fundamental problem of causal inference), we focus on the average causal effect (ATE) in a population:

\[ \text{Average Causal Effect} \iff E[Y^{a=1}] \neq E[Y^{a=0}] \]

Association, on the other hand, is about comparing observed outcomes in different groups of people[cite: 96].

\[ \text{Association} \iff E[Y|A=1] \neq E[Y|A=0] \]

The crucial link between these two concepts is the assumption of exchangeability, which we will discuss extensively. For now, understand that if the exposed and unexposed groups are exchangeable (i.e., they are comparable in every way except for the exposure), then the association we measure will equal the causal effect we seek. An ideal randomized trial achieves this. Our job with observational data is to try to achieve it through careful study design and analysis.

Quantifying Association and Causal Effects

Whether we are describing an association or a causal effect, we use the same set of epidemiological measures. The only difference is the notation and, most importantly, the assumptions that justify the interpretation.

For the following, let’s consider a binary outcome \(Y\) (1=event, 0=no event), where the mean \(E[Y]\) is simply the probability of the event, \(\Prob(Y=1)\), which we often call the risk.

Risk Difference (RD)

  • Intuitive Understanding: The RD tells us the absolute difference in risk between two groups. It answers the question: “How many more (or fewer) cases of the outcome are there in the exposed group compared to the unexposed group, out of every 100 people?” It’s a useful measure for public health because it directly translates to the number of cases that could be prevented.
  • Mathematical Foundations:
    • Associational Risk Difference: \(RD_{Assoc} = \Prob(Y=1|A=1) - \Prob(Y=1|A=0)\) [cite: 84]
    • Causal Risk Difference: \(RD_{Causal} = \Prob(Y^{a=1}=1) - \Prob(Y^{a=0}=1)\) [cite: 57]

Risk Ratio (RR)

  • Intuitive Understanding: The RR, also called relative risk, tells us the relative difference in risk. It answers the question: “How many times more (or less) likely are exposed individuals to develop the outcome compared to unexposed individuals?” It’s often used to measure the strength of an association. An RR of 2 means the risk is “doubled,” while an RR of 0.5 means the risk is “halved.”
  • Mathematical Foundations:
    • Associational Risk Ratio: \(RR_{Assoc} = \frac{\Prob(Y=1|A=1)}{\Prob(Y=1|A=0)}\) [cite: 84]
    • Causal Risk Ratio: \(RR_{Causal} = \frac{\Prob(Y^{a=1}=1)}{\Prob(Y^{a=0}=1)}\) [cite: 57]

Odds And the Odds Ratio (OR)

  • Intuitive Understanding: This one is less intuitive. The odds of an event is the probability of it happening divided by the probability of it not happening. If the probability of an event is 0.75 (or 75%), the odds are \(0.75 / (1-0.75) = 3\), often stated as “3 to 1.” The Odds Ratio (OR) is simply the ratio of the odds in the exposed group to the odds in the unexposed group. A key property is that when the outcome is rare, the OR is a good approximation of the RR.
  • Mathematical Foundations:
    • The odds of an event is \(Odds = \frac{\Prob(Y=1)}{\Prob(Y=0)} = \frac{p}{1-p}\).
    • Associational Odds Ratio: \(OR_{Assoc} = \frac{\Prob(Y=1|A=1) / \Prob(Y=0|A=1)}{\Prob(Y=1|A=0) / \Prob(Y=0|A=0)}\) [cite: 85]
    • Causal Odds Ratio: \(OR_{Causal} = \frac{\Prob(Y^{a=1}=1) / \Prob(Y^{a=1}=0)}{\Prob(Y^{a=0}=1) / \Prob(Y^{a=0}=0)}\) [cite: 57]

Measures for Survival Analysis

When our outcome is the time until an event occurs (e.g., death, disease diagnosis), we use slightly different measures, as detailed in HR Chapter 17[cite: 892].

Survival Probability and Risk (Cumulative Incidence)

  • Intuitive Understanding: The survival probability at time \(k\), denoted \(S(k)\), is the probability of not having the event by that time (i.e., “surviving” past time \(k\)). A survival curve plots this probability over time. The risk, or cumulative incidence, at time \(k\), denoted \(F(k)\), is the opposite: the probability of having had the event by that time.
  • Mathematical Foundations:
    • Let \(T\) be the time to the event.
    • Survival Probability: \(S(k) = \Prob(T \> k)\) [cite: 893]
    • Risk / Cumulative Incidence: \(F(k) = \Prob(T \le k) = 1 - S(k)\) [cite: 893]

Hazard

  • Intuitive Understanding: The hazard is the instantaneous potential for the event to occur at time \(k\), given that an individual has survived up to that point. Think of it as the risk of the event happening right now, among those who are still at risk. Unlike cumulative risk which can only increase, the hazard can go up or down over time.
  • Mathematical Foundations:
    • For discrete time (e.g., months), the hazard at month \(k\) is the conditional probability: \(h(k) = \Prob(T=k | T \> k-1)\)[cite: 895].

These measures form the basis for comparing groups in survival analysis, often through survival curves or hazard ratios.

This concludes our foundational overview. The next crucial step is to understand the assumptions that allow us to move from measuring associations in our data to estimating these causal quantities.

Let’s simulate a small dataset to see how this works in R.

Scenario: We will simulate a population where a treatment A has a true causal effect on a binary outcome Y. We’ll start with a simple case: a randomized trial where there is no confounding.

R Syntax Explanation:

  • set.seed(579): This function ensures that every time you run the code, the “random” numbers generated will be the same. This is crucial for making our results reproducible.
  • rbinom(n, 1, 0.5): This generates n random numbers from a binomial distribution. Here, it’s like flipping a coin n times, where the probability of getting a 1 (heads) is 0.5. This simulates our random treatment assignment.
  • ifelse(test, yes, no): This is a conditional statement. It checks if treatment == 1. If true, it assigns the value 0.25; if false, it assigns 0.10. This is how we build the true causal effect into our simulation.
  • %>%: This is the “pipe” operator from the dplyr package (part of tidyverse). It takes the output of the expression on its left and “pipes” it in as the first argument to the function on its right. It makes the code easier to read.
  • group_by(A): This tells R to perform the next set of operations separately for each group defined by the variable A (treated and untreated).
  • summarise(…): This creates a new data frame with summary statistics. n() counts the rows, sum(Y) counts the events, and mean(Y) calculates the risk.
  • cat(…): This function is like print(), but gives us more control over formatting the output text. The \n creates a new line.

Interpretation of R Output:

When you run this code, you will see that the associational measures you calculated from the simulated data are very close to the true causal effects we built into the simulation (Associational RD ≈ 0.15, Associational RR ≈ 2.5). This is because we simulated a perfect, infinitely large randomized trial. In this ideal scenario, where the treated and untreated groups are exchangeable by design, the association we measure is a consistent estimate of the causal effect.

Part A: Conceptual Questions

  1. Association vs. Causation: Let’s consider the observed association between drinking coffee (\(A\)) and heart disease (\(Y\)). Many studies show coffee drinkers have a higher risk. However, coffee drinkers are also more likely to be smokers (\(L\)). Smoking is a known cause of heart disease.   2. Choosing a Measure:

    • Intervention 1:
      • Risk in unexposed = 4/100 = 0.04. Risk in exposed = 2/100 = 0.02.
      • RR: 0.02 / 0.04 = 0.5 (The risk is halved).
      • RD: 0.02 - 0.04 = -0.02 (Prevents 2 cases per 100 people).
    • Intervention 2:
      • Risk in unexposed = 40/100 = 0.40. Risk in exposed = 30/100 = 0.30.
      • RR: 0.30 / 0.40 = 0.75 (A 25% risk reduction).
      • RD: 0.30 - 0.40 = -0.10 (Prevents 10 cases per 100 people).
    • Interpretation: The Risk Difference (RD) better reflects public health impact, as Intervention 2 prevents 5 times as many cases (10 vs. 2 per 100). The Risk Ratio (RR) better reflects the relative strength of the effect, as Intervention 1 cuts the risk in half, a stronger relative effect than the 25% reduction from Intervention 2.

  3. Counterfactuals: It’s impossible to know because we cannot observe the counterfactual outcome: what would have happened to that specific patient at that specific time if they had not taken the aspirin. Maybe the headache was going to resolve on its own at that exact moment. This is the fundamental problem of causal inference.

Part B: Computation from a Table

  1. Risks:

    • Risk in treated: \(\Prob(Y=1|A=1) = 80 / 1000 = 0.08\) (or 8%).
    • Risk in untreated: \(\Prob(Y=1|A=0) = 50 / 1000 = 0.05\) (or 5%).

  2. Risk Difference (RD):

    • \(RD = 0.08 - 0.05 = 0.03\).
    • Interpretation: There are 3 additional cases of stroke for every 100 people treated with the drug compared to those not treated.

  3. Risk Ratio (RR):

    • \(RR = 0.08 / 0.05 = 1.6\).
    • Interpretation: People who take the drug are 1.6 times as likely to have a stroke as people who do not.

  4. Odds Ratio (OR):

    • Odds in treated: \((80/1000) / (920/1000) = 80/920 \approx 0.087\).
    • Odds in untreated: \((50/1000) / (950/1000) = 50/950 \approx 0.053\).
    • \(OR = (80/920) / (50/950) = (80 \times 950) / (920 \times 50) \approx 1.65\).
    • Interpretation: The odds of having a stroke are 65% higher in the treated group compared to the untreated group. (Note that because the outcome is relatively rare, the OR of 1.65 is close to the RR of 1.6).

Part A: Conceptual Questions

  1. Association vs. Causation: Let’s consider the observed association between owning a yellow car (A) and being in a car accident (Y). Data might show that owners of yellow cars have a lower risk of accidents. However, it’s unlikely the yellow paint itself provides a physical shield. A more plausible explanation is that people who choose to buy a yellow car (A) may be more cautious drivers in general (L). This personality trait of being cautious (L) is a common cause of both choosing a yellow car and having a lower risk of an accident. The association is real, but it is confounded by driver caution.

    • Causal Diagram: In this diagram, an unmeasured variable L (Cautious Personality) has an arrow pointing to A (Buys Yellow Car) and an arrow pointing to Y (Car Accident). There is no direct arrow from A to Y.

  2. Choosing a Measure:

    • Intervention 1:

      • Risk in unexposed = 4/100 = 0.04. Risk in exposed = 2/100 = 0.02.

      • RD: 0.02 - 0.04 = -0.02 (Prevents 2 cases per 100 people).
