Introduction

Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) is a cornerstone of statistical analysis for comparing means across groups, applicable to fixed effects, random effects, and mixed effects models. The choice of method depends on the experimental design (balanced or unbalanced), the nature of the effects (fixed, random, or mixed), and the analysis goal (e.g., hypothesis testing or variance component estimation). This document provides two flowcharts to guide researchers in selecting appropriate methods, along with corresponding R syntax and packages. The flowcharts correct common pitfalls, emphasizing aov() for balanced designs, lm() for unbalanced fixed effects, and lme4::lmer() for unbalanced random or mixed effects models.

Flowchart

The flowchart below illustrates the decision-making process for selecting ANOVA methods based on the type of model and design. The flowchart is divided into two main branches: one for fixed effects models and another for random or mixed effects models. Each branch further splits based on whether the design is balanced or unbalanced, leading to specific R functions and packages for analysis.

Explanation of the Flowcharts

The flowcharts guide the selection of ANOVA methods based on three key decision points:

  1. Model Type:
    • Fixed Effects: Factors with specific, repeatable levels (e.g., treatment types). Focus on comparing means.
    • Random or Mixed Effects: Random effects represent a sample from a population (e.g., subjects, blocks), and mixed models combine fixed and random effects. Focus includes variance component estimation.
  2. Design Type:
    • Balanced: Equal sample sizes across all groups (e.g., same number of replicates per treatment).
    • Unbalanced: Unequal sample sizes, common in observational studies or with missing data.
  3. Analysis Goal (for Unbalanced Random/Mixed Models):
    • Variance Component Estimation: Estimating variances of random effects (e.g., \(\sigma_a^2\)).
    • Hypothesis Testing on Fixed Effects: Testing significance of fixed effects in mixed models.
    • Confidence Intervals for Variance Components: Quantifying uncertainty in variance estimates.

Flowchart Logic

  • Fixed Effects Models:
    • Balanced: Use aov(response ~ factor, data) from the stats package for classical ANOVA. The Error() term can be included for blocking or randomization structures.
    • Unbalanced: Use lm(response ~ factor, data) with car::Anova(lm_object, type="III") to handle unequal sample sizes using Type III sums of squares, which are robust to unbalanced designs.
  • Random or Mixed Effects Models:
    • Balanced:
      • Use aov(response ~ factor + Error(random_factor), data) to specify random effects strata.
      • Estimate variance components manually using Expected Mean Squares (EMS), e.g., \(\hat{\sigma}_a^2 = \frac{\text{MS}_A - \text{MS}_\text{Error}}{n}\).
    • Unbalanced:
      • Variance Component Estimation: Use lmer(response ~ fixed + (1|random), data, REML=TRUE) from lme4 for REML estimation, which is less biased than maximum likelihood (ML).
      • Hypothesis Testing on Fixed Effects: Use lmerTest::anova(lmer_model) to apply Kenward-Roger or Satterthwaite approximations for F-tests, accounting for unbalanced data.
      • Confidence Intervals for Variance Components: Use confint(lmer_model) for profile likelihood intervals or bootMer() for bootstrapping, both from lme4.

R Packages

  • stats: Built-in R package for aov() and lm(), ideal for balanced designs and simple linear models.
  • car: Provides Anova() for Type III sums of squares in unbalanced fixed effects models.
  • lme4: Preferred for mixed effects models, supporting unbalanced designs, random slopes, and REML estimation.
  • lmerTest: Extends lme4 with Kenward-Roger and Satterthwaite F-tests for hypothesis testing.
  • nlme: Alternative to lme4 for complex correlation structures or variance functions.
