The goal when reading a methodological paper is not to memorize every formula but to build a robust mental model of the author’s argument. You want to understand the problem they solved, the logic of their solution, the assumptions required for it to work, and its ultimate impact. This active, model-building approach is far more effective than passive reading.

We will use a Three-Pass Approach designed to manage cognitive load and build your understanding from a scaffold to a fully-formed structure.

Pass 1: The Bird’s-Eye View – Building the Scaffold (15 Minutes) 🗺️

Don’t start by reading page one. Your first goal is to create a mental “map” of the paper’s purpose and structure. This provides a scaffold for your brain to hang the details on later, which significantly reduces cognitive load.

  1. Read the Abstract, Introduction, and Conclusion First: Read these three sections carefully. At the end of this step, you should be able to answer, in simple terms:
    • The Problem: What specific challenge or gap in knowledge existed before this paper was written?
    • The Solution: What is the main idea, method, or framework this paper proposes?
    • The Claim: What do the authors claim is the primary impact or takeaway of their work?
  2. Scan the Section Headings: Look at the structure of the paper. How do the authors build their argument from the problem to the solution?
  3. Examine Figures and Conceptual Tables: Look at any diagrams (like DAGs), charts, or conceptual tables. Read their captions. These often contain the most important ideas in a condensed, visual format.

At the end of this pass, you shouldn’t understand the details, but you should have a clear sense of the paper’s story.

Pass 2: The Deep Dive – Adding the Bricks with Active Recall (1-2 Hours) 🧱

Now, read the paper from start to finish, but with an active, questioning mindset. Your goal is to deconstruct the argument, not just consume it.

  1. “Translate” the Math: When you encounter a block of equations, your goal is not to re-derive the proofs. Instead, focus on the English sentences before and after the math. Your task is to translate the mathematical statement into a conceptual one. For example, when you see the formula for the propensity score, you should translate it in your notes as: “This is the probability of getting the treatment, given the measured covariates L.”
  2. Focus on the Assumptions: For causal inference papers, assumptions are the most important part. They are the weak points of any argument. When an assumption is stated (e.g., “unconfoundedness,” “exclusion restriction”), stop and ask:
    • What does this assumption mean conceptually?
    • Why is it necessary for the author’s argument to work?
    • Is this assumption testable? If not, in what real-world scenarios might it be plausible or implausible?
  3. Engage in Active Recall: After reading each major section, stop, look away from the page, and ask yourself: “What was the single most important point of that section?” Try to summarize it in one or two sentences, either in your head or in your notes. This act of forcing your brain to retrieve the information is far more powerful for learning than passively re-reading the text.

Pass 3: The Feynman Technique – Solidifying Understanding (30 Minutes) ✍️

You only truly understand something when you can explain it simply. This final pass solidifies your mental model and reveals any remaining gaps in your knowledge.

  1. Put the paper away. Open a blank document or grab a piece of paper.
  2. Explain the paper to an imaginary first-year graduate student. Your explanation must clearly and simply answer the following five questions:
    • What was the central problem the authors were trying to solve?
    • What was their key insight or proposed solution?
    • In simple terms, what is the core logic for why their solution works?
    • What are the 1-3 critical assumptions it relies on to be valid?
    • What is the single biggest limitation or most important implication of this work?

If you get stuck or find yourself using jargon you can’t define, you’ve found the edge of your understanding. Go back to the paper and focus specifically on that part. This technique is the most efficient way to build deep, lasting comprehension.

Example Application: The Feynman Technique for Week 5 (Rosenbaum & Rubin, 1983)

After reading the paper, your Feynman explanation might look like this:

  • Problem: In observational studies, people who get a treatment are different from those who don’t across many variables (confounders). Trying to adjust for all of them in a single regression model is complicated and non-transparent.
  • Solution: They proposed the propensity score—the probability of getting treated given your set of covariates. They proved you only need to adjust for this single number to remove bias from all observed confounders.
  • Logic: The propensity score has a “balancing property.” At any given value of the score, the distribution of the covariates is the same between the treated and untreated groups, just like in a randomized experiment.
  • Assumptions: This only works if you’ve measured all the important confounders (unconfoundedness) and if there are both treated and untreated people at every level of the propensity score (positivity/overlap). Unconfoundedness is a huge, untestable assumption.
  • Impact: This revolutionized observational research by creating a two-step process: first, focus on study design by creating balanced groups, and only then, perform the outcome analysis. Its biggest limitation is that it does nothing to solve the problem of unmeasured confounders.
