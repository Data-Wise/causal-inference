Instructions

Each week, you are assigned two papers: a Classic paper that established a foundational concept and a Modern paper that extends, applies, or reframes that concept. You are expected to be familiar with both for our class discussions.

For each paper, read the provided summary and prepare written responses to the three guiding questions. Bring your notes to class to support discussion and critiques.

Part I: Foundations

Week 1 – Causal Effects & SUTVA

  • Classic: Rubin, D. B. (1974). Estimating causal effects of treatments in randomized and nonrandomized studies. Journal of Educational Psychology, 66(5), 688–701. https://doi.org/10.1037/h0037350

    Summary: Introduces the potential outcomes framework, defines causal effects as counterfactual contrasts, and articulates the “fundamental problem of causal inference.”

    Questions:

    1. In your own words, what is the fundamental problem of causal inference?
    2. Why is the Stable Unit Treatment Value Assumption (SUTVA) necessary? Give a real-world example of violation.
    3. How does this framework clarify the difference between causal effects and observed associations?

  • Modern: Rubin, D. B. (2005). Causal inference using potential outcomes: Design, modeling, decisions. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 100(469), 322–331. https://doi.org/10.1198/016214504000001880

    Summary: Reflects on the evolution of the framework, emphasizing “design trumps analysis” and transparency of assumptions.

    Questions:

    1. What does Rubin mean by “design trumps analysis”? Provide an example.
    2. How does the framework make assumptions explicit compared to regression?
    3. What challenges arise when prioritizing design in observational studies?

Week 2 – DAGs

  • Classic: Greenland, S., Pearl, J., & Robins, J. M. (1999). Causal diagrams for epidemiologic research. Epidemiology, 10(1), 37–48. https://doi.org/10.1097/00001648-199901000-00008

    Summary: Introduces Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) as a language for representing causal assumptions and identifying confounding, colliders, and valid adjustment sets.

    Questions:

    1. Draw a DAG for your own research area, identifying one confounder, one mediator, and one collider.
    2. What is the back-door criterion, and why does it justify covariate adjustment?
    3. Why is conditioning on a collider problematic? Provide an example.

  • Modern: Textor, J., van der Zander, B., Gilthorpe, M. S., Liśkiewicz, M., & Ellison, G. T. H. (2016). Robust causal inference using directed acyclic graphs: The R package dagitty. International Journal of Epidemiology, 45(6), 1887–1894. https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyw341

    Summary: Introduces dagitty, a tool to automate adjustment set selection, evaluate testable implications, and visualize DAGs.

    Questions:

    1. What are the advantages of using dagitty versus manual identification?
    2. How do testable implications help assess a DAG?
    3. What limitations remain in relying on software for causal modeling?

Week 3 – Exchangeability / Positivity / Consistency

  • Classic: Holland, P. W. (1986). Statistics and causal inference. Journal of the American Statistical Association, 81(396), 945–960. https://doi.org/10.1080/01621459.1986.10478354

    Summary: Clarifies Rubin’s model by distinguishing association vs. causation, stressing “no causation without manipulation,” and outlining assumptions of exchangeability, positivity, and consistency.

    Questions:

    1. How does “no causation without manipulation” distinguish causes from attributes?
    2. What are the three core assumptions for causal inference?
    3. Which assumption is hardest to verify in practice, and why?

  • Modern: Hernán, M. A. (2018). The C-word: Scientific euphemisms do not improve causal inference from observational data. American Journal of Public Health, 108(5), 616–619. https://doi.org/10.2105/AJPH.2018.304337

    Summary: Critiques euphemisms like “associated with,” arguing that explicit causal language enforces clarity about assumptions.

    Questions:

    1. What risks arise from using causal euphemisms?
    2. Do you agree with Hernán that researchers should always say “cause”? Why or why not?
    3. When might it be valid to avoid causal language?

Week 4 – Confounding & Selection Bias

  • Classic: Hernán, M. A., Hernández-Díaz, S., & Robins, J. M. (2004). A structural approach to selection bias. Epidemiology, 15(5), 615–625. https://doi.org/10.1097/01.ede.0000135174.63482.43

    Summary: Provides a unified framework for selection bias using DAGs, showing how many biases (Berkson’s bias, loss-to-follow-up) arise from conditioning on a collider.

    Questions:

    1. Draw a DAG illustrating how restricting to hospitalized patients induces spurious associations.
    2. How can loss-to-follow-up be represented in a DAG, and under what conditions does it bias estimates?
    3. Why is collider bias a unifying explanation for many selection biases?

  • Modern: Cole, S. R., & Hernán, M. A. (2002). Fallibility in estimating direct effects. International Journal of Epidemiology, 31(1), 163–165. https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/31.1.163

    Summary: Demonstrates via DAGs that adjusting for mediators with regression can misestimate direct effects if there is unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding.

    Questions:

    1. Why does the standard regression approach to mediation often fail?
    2. How does unmeasured mediator–outcome confounding complicate direct effect estimation?
    3. How does this set the stage for modern causal mediation analysis?

Part II: Estimation Methods

Week 5 – Propensity Scores

Classic : Rosenbaum, P. R., & Rubin, D. B. (1983). The central role of the propensity score in observational studies for causal effects. Biometrika, 70(1), 41–55. https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/70.1.41. Stable links: OUP; JSTOR. Oxford Academic JSTOR

Summary.
Proves that the propensity score is a balancing score, and that conditioning on it suffices to remove bias from observed covariates under strong ignorability. Lays foundations for matching, subclassification, IPTW, and covariate adjustment via the score. 24Oxford Academic

Critique questions.

  1. Provide diagnostics to verify the balancing property in finite samples—what failures most threaten bias/variance?
  2. Under which sampling regimes does trimming (lack of overlap) improve MSE yet alter the estimand?
  3. Discuss the limits of propensity scores under interference or measurement error in covariates.

Modern:

Austin, P. C. (2011). An introduction to propensity score methods for reducing the effects of confounding in observational studies. Multivariate Behavioral Research, 46(3), 399–424. https://doi.org/10.1080/00273171.2011.568786. Stable links: PubMed/PMC. 25PubMed PMC

Summary.
Reviews four implementations (matching, stratification, IPTW, regression adjustment) and stresses balance diagnostics and overlap checks. Offers pragmatic guidance for implementation choices and reporting. PMC

Critique questions.

  1. Compare nearest-neighbor matching vs. IPTW for ATE/ATT under severe imbalance; which estimand is recovered, and at what variance cost?
  2. What constitutes adequate common support? Propose quantitative thresholds and a pre-registered trimming rule.
  3. Evaluate post-matching outcome modeling: when does it help vs. reintroduce model dependence?

Week 6 – IPTW & Marginal Structural Models

  • Classic:
    Robins, J. M., Hernán, M. A., & Brumback, B. (2000). Marginal structural models and causal inference in epidemiology. Epidemiology, 11(5), 550–560. https://doi.org/10.1097/00001648-200009000-00011

    Summary: Introduces MSMs and IPTW to adjust for time-varying confounding affected by prior treatment.

    Questions:

    1. Why does standard regression fail when confounders are also mediators?
    2. What is the pseudo-population created by IPTW, and how does it help?
    3. What are key limitations of MSMs in practice?

  • Modern: Cole, S. R., & Hernán, M. A. (2008). Constructing inverse probability weights for marginal structural models. American Journal of Epidemiology, 168(6), 656–664. https://doi.org/10.1093/aje/kwn164

    Summary: Provides step-by-step guidance on constructing stabilized IPTWs and diagnostics.

    Questions:

    1. Why are stabilized weights preferred over unstabilized?
    2. What diagnostic checks are necessary after weighting?
    3. How do extreme weights undermine MSM results?

Week 7 – G-computation & TMLE

  • Classic: Robins, J. M. (1986). A new approach to causal inference in mortality studies with sustained exposure periods—application of the G-formula. Mathematical Modelling, 7(9–12), 1393–1512. https://doi.org/10.1016/0270-0255(86)90088-6

    Summary: Introduces the g-formula for estimating causal effects of time-varying exposures using outcome modeling under interventions.

    Questions:

    1. How does g-computation differ from IPTW?
    2. What is sequential exchangeability, and why is it critical?
    3. What are the pros/cons of outcome-modeling approaches?

  • Modern: Petersen, M. L., & van der Laan, M. J. (2014). Causal models and learning from data: Integrating causal modeling and statistical estimation. Epidemiology, 25(3), 418–426. https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000000078

    Summary: Describes Targeted Maximum Likelihood Estimation (TMLE), a doubly robust method combining causal models with efficient estimation.

    Questions:

    1. What does “doubly robust” mean in practice?
    2. How does TMLE improve on g-computation?
    3. Why separate identification from estimation in causal analysis?

Week 8 – Instrumental Variables

  • Classic: Angrist, J. D. (1990). Lifetime earnings and the Vietnam era draft lottery: Evidence from social security administrative records. American Economic Review, 80(3), 313–336. http://www.jstor.org/stable/2006669

    Summary: Uses the Vietnam draft lottery as an instrument to estimate the causal effect of military service on earnings.

    Questions:

    1. What three assumptions must hold for a valid instrument?
    2. Who are the “compliers,” and why does IV identify their effect?
    3. Which IV assumption is least plausible here?

  • Modern: Angrist, J. D., & Pischke, J.-S. (2010). The credibility revolution in empirical economics. Journal of Economic Perspectives, 24(2), 3–30. https://doi.org/10.1257/jep.24.2.3

    Summary: Argues that modern applied economics has shifted to emphasize credible research designs (IV, RD, DiD).

    Questions:

    1. What is the “credibility revolution”?
    2. How does design alter the role of modeling in causal inference?
    3. What risks exist in over-reliance on quasi-experiments?

Part III: Advanced Topics

Week 9 – Effect Modification & Interaction

  • Classic:
    Rothman, K. J. (1974). Synergy and antagonism in cause–effect relationships. American Journal of Epidemiology, 99(6), 385–388. https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordjournals.aje.a121617

    Summary: Introduces the sufficient-component cause (“causal pies”) model, framing synergy and antagonism among causes.

    Questions:

    1. How can two factors show additive but not multiplicative interaction?
    2. How does the causal pie model explain effect heterogeneity?
    3. Why is additive interaction often more relevant in public health?

  • Modern: VanderWeele, T. J. (2019). Principles of effect modification and interaction. International Journal of Epidemiology, 48(1), 301–308. https://doi.org/10.1093/ije/dyy276

    Summary: Clarifies interaction concepts, advocating for additive-scale measures and subgroup reporting.

    Questions:

    1. Why report subgroup-specific effects instead of only an interaction p-value?
    2. Distinguish “effect modification” from “statistical interaction.”
    3. How does effect modification inform policy?

Week 10 – Mediation I

  • Classic:
    Baron, R. M., & Kenny, D. A. (1986). The moderator–mediator variable distinction in social psychological research. Journal of Personality and Social Psychology, 51(6), 1173–1182. https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-3514.51.6.1173

    Summary: Proposes the traditional four-step regression-based framework for mediation.

    Questions:

    1. What are the four Baron–Kenny steps?
    2. What hidden assumptions are needed for causal interpretation?
    3. Why is this approach now considered insufficient?

  • Modern: Imai, K., Keele, L., & Tingley, D. (2010). A general approach to causal mediation analysis. Psychological Methods, 15(4), 309–334. https://doi.org/10.1037/a0020761

    Summary: Develops a potential outcomes framework for mediation, defining natural direct/indirect effects under sequential ignorability.

    Questions:

    1. What is sequential ignorability, and why is it strong?
    2. How do natural effects improve on Baron–Kenny?
    3. What challenges arise in applying this approach?

Week 11 – Mediation II & Principal Stratification

  • Classic:
    Robins, J. M., & Greenland, S. (1992). Identifiability and exchangeability for direct and indirect effects. Epidemiology, 3(2), 143–155. https://doi.org/10.1097/00001648-199203000-00013

    Summary: Provides counterfactual definitions of direct/indirect effects, requiring sequential exchangeability and no exposure–mediator interaction.

    Questions:

    1. What are the two parts of sequential exchangeability?
    2. Why does exposure–mediator interaction block nonparametric identification?
    3. How does this extend mediation beyond Baron–Kenny?

  • Modern: Daniel, R. M., De Stavola, B. L., Cousens, S. N., & Vansteelandt, S. (2015). Causal mediation analysis with multiple mediators. Epidemiology, 26(3), 364–372. https://doi.org/10.1097/EDE.0000000000000253

    Summary: Extends mediation analysis to multiple, possibly correlated mediators, introducing assumptions for path-specific effects.

    Questions:

    1. Why can’t indirect effects from separate single-mediator models be summed?
    2. What new assumptions are needed for path-specific effects?
    3. How does this reflect real-world complexity?

Week 12 – Sensitivity Analysis

  • Classic:
    Rosenbaum, P. R. (1987). Sensitivity analysis for matched observational studies. Biometrika, 74(1), 13–26. https://doi.org/10.1093/biomet/74.1.13

    Summary: Introduces sensitivity analysis for unmeasured confounding in matched studies, quantifying how strong a hidden confounder must be to change conclusions.

    Questions:

    1. What is the key idea behind Rosenbaum’s sensitivity analysis?
    2. Why is sensitivity analysis critical in observational causal studies?
    3. How might this framework be adapted for non-matched designs?

  • Modern: VanderWeele, T. J., & Ding, P. (2017). Sensitivity analysis in observational research: Introducing the E-value. Annals of Internal Medicine, 167(4), 268–274. https://doi.org/10.7326/M16-2607

    Summary: Proposes the E-value, a simple measure of how strong an unmeasured confounder would need to be to explain away an observed association.

    Questions:

    1. How do you interpret an E-value of 3.0 for a risk ratio of 2.0?
    2. What are the assumptions behind the E-value?
    3. What are its limitations compared to full sensitivity analyses?

Week 13 – Target Trial Emulation

  • Classic: Robins, J. M. (1987). A graphical approach to the identification and estimation of causal parameters in mortality studies with sustained exposure periods. Journal of Chronic Diseases, 40(Suppl. 2), 139S–161S. https://doi.org/10.1016/S0021-9681(87)80033-0

    Summary: Early graphical framework showing how observational data can emulate randomized trials with sequential treatments.

    Questions:

    1. Why emulate a randomized trial with observational data?
    2. What is this paper’s key conceptual contribution?
    3. How do DAGs/graphs assist in this setting?

  • Modern: Hernán, M. A., Alonso, A., Logan, R., Grodstein, F., Michels, K. B., Willett, W. C., Manson, J. E., & Robins, J. M. (2008). Specifying a target trial prevents immortal time bias and other self-inflicted injuries in observational analyses. Journal of Clinical Epidemiology, 61(1), 41–45. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jclinepi.2007.03.014

    Summary: Outlines the target trial framework, showing how explicitly defining trial components prevents immortal time bias.

    Questions:

    1. What is immortal time bias, and how does a target trial prevent it?
    2. What are the seven key components of a target trial protocol?
    3. How does target trial emulation improve transparency?
